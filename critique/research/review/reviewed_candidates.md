# Video 1: Sol-reviewed candidate set

Status 29 September 2026: **REVIEWED**. Fifteen candidates are recorded below, including one unreviewed preprint explicitly excluded from the published-evidence pool and one abstract-only reserve. Thirteen published sources had relevant full-text passages checked directly by Sol; Chu was checked in a complete public copy whose distribution permission is unknown, and Loftus–Pickrell in a near-final author manuscript. “Full text checked” means the relevant article sections, methods/results, and limitations were inspected, not that the study proves the video’s causal claim. Selection and detailed QA are in [review_audit.md](review_audit.md). The question is whether inability to remember a *possible* traumatic event is *usually* due to protective blocking.

## Williams (1994) — documented event, later nonreport

Williams, L. M. (1994). Recall of childhood trauma: A prospective study of women's memories of child sexual abuse. *Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology, 62*(6), 1167–1176. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-006X.62.6.1167. [Full article](https://www.memoiretraumatique.org/assets/files/v1/Documents-pdf/1994-Anglais-Souvenir-du-traumatisme-de-l-enfance-Linda-M-Williams.pdf); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7860814/). Published, peer-reviewed empirical cohort follow-up. **Full text read; numerical and interpretive claims independently verified** at pp. 1167, 1170–1172 (Fig. 1, Tables 1–3), 1173–1175.

129 adult women with hospital-documented childhood sexual victimization were interviewed about 17 years later. 49/129 (38%) did not report/recollect the index abuse; 80/129 did. Younger age and perpetrator familiarity were associated with nonreport. Crucial nuance: most were at least 7 at abuse; among those aged 7 or older, 28% had no recall (p. 1171, Table 1). The author tested some reluctance/record-error explanations, but could not directly observe the current accessibility of the memory. **Bearing:** strong evidence that later nonreport of documented abuse happens; no direct test of protective blocking, so it cannot establish that this is the usual cause of an uncertain person's gap. Avoid calling the 38% “repressed.”

## Williams (1995) — same cohort, retrospective forgotten interval

Williams, L. M. (1995). Recovered memories of abuse in women with documented child sexual victimization histories. *Journal of Traumatic Stress, 8*(4), 649–673. https://doi.org/10.1002/jts.2490080408. [Full article](https://www.jimhopper.com/pdf/williams1995.pdf); [publisher](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/jts.2490080408). Published, peer-reviewed empirical follow-up of **the same 129 women** as Williams (1994), so one evidence family. **Full text read; claims verified** at pp. 649, 655–656, 662–663 (Table 6), 668–670.

Of 80 who recalled index abuse, 16% (about one in ten of the total 129) retrospectively reported a past interval of forgetting. On measured discrepancies against earlier records, recovered and continuous rememberers did not differ (average 2.00 vs 1.88 inconsistent details; p. 662, Table 6). The comparison is small and retrospective; a similar discrepancy count does not validate every detail, and the original report can also contain errors. **Bearing:** some delayed memories concern documented events; memory discontinuity alone does not mean fabrication. It does not identify why the earlier gap occurred.

## Geraerts et al. (2007) — independent corroboration by recovery context

Geraerts, E., Schooler, J. W., Merckelbach, H., Jelicic, M., Hauer, B. J. A., & Ambadar, Z. (2007). The reality of recovered memories: Corroborating continuous and discontinuous memories of childhood sexual abuse. *Psychological Science, 18*(7), 564–568. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2007.01940.x. [Full article](https://sites.pitt.edu/~bertsch/Geraerts.pdf). Published, peer-reviewed empirical study. **Full text read; claims verified** at pp. 565–567, especially Method, Results, Table 1 and Discussion.

Newspaper-recruited, self-selected participants: 71 reported continuous memories; 57 reported discontinuous memories, split into spontaneous recall outside therapy (n=41) and recall in therapy (n=16). Blind raters sought reports from others meeting prespecified corroboration criteria. Corroboration: 45% continuous, 37% outside-therapy recovery, 0/16 therapy recovery (p. 565). This supports possible accuracy of some spontaneous delayed memories and a context difference. The authors explicitly state that uncorroborated does **not** imply false; corroboration itself is imperfect, and remembered discontinuity is self-reported. Recovery context was not randomized; therapy-associated differences cannot be attributed solely to therapist suggestion. **Bearing:** later accuracy varies; it cannot diagnose protective blocking or estimate its usual frequency.

## Geraerts et al. (2009) — heterogeneous cognitive profiles

Geraerts, E., Lindsay, D. S., Merckelbach, H., Jelicic, M., Raymaekers, L., Arnold, M. M., & Schooler, J. W. (2009). Cognitive mechanisms underlying recovered-memory experiences of childhood sexual abuse. *Psychological Science, 20*(1), 92–98. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02247.x. [Full article](https://onlineacademiccommunity.uvic.ca/lindsaylab/wp-content/uploads/sites/4861/2020/07/GeraertsLindsayMerkelbachJelicieRaymaekersArnoldSchooler2008.pdf); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19037903/). Published, peer-reviewed empirical comparison. **Full text read; methods and results verified** at pp. 93–96, Method, DRM/FIA task descriptions, Results/Tables 1–2, Discussion.

120 adults recruited by newspaper advertisement, 30 each in spontaneous recovery, suggestive-therapy recovery, continuous-memory, and no-abuse control groups. The suggestive-therapy group had greater *laboratory* false-word recall susceptibility; the spontaneous group more often forgot previous *laboratory* acts of remembering. Groups were classified retrospectively, and the tasks neither verify the abuse reports nor measure actual causal mechanisms of any earlier gap. The suggestive group specifically involved hypnosis/guided imagery/dream interpretation (p. 93); it is not a generic therapy comparison. **Bearing:** multiple plausible cognitive routes to a reported recovery; not a unitary protective-blocking explanation.

## Loftus & Pickrell (1995) — suggestive false autobiographical reports

Loftus, E. F., & Pickrell, J. E. (1995). The formation of false memories. *Psychiatric Annals, 25*(12), 720–725. https://doi.org/10.3928/0048-5713-19951201-07. [Author manuscript close to published version](https://www.myptsd.com/gallery/-pdf/1-96.pdf); [publisher record](https://journals.healio.com/doi/10.3928/0048-5713-19951201-07). Published, peer-reviewed empirical experiment. **Near-final full manuscript read; precise article wording/figures not independently checked against final typeset version.** Relevant manuscript sections: “Lost again,” Method, Results, Discussion (PDF pp. 2–5); publication pp. 720–725.

24 adults heard three true childhood narratives and one false “lost in a mall/public place” narrative ostensibly supplied by a family member, then completed a booklet and two interviews. Six of 24 (25%) still claimed some full **or partial** recollection after interviews; partial reports included speculation, and their clarity/detail were generally lower than true memories. The design demonstrates susceptibility under deliberate family-backed suggestion, not prevalence of false trauma memories in ordinary therapy. **Bearing:** later felt recollection is not automatic proof of event truth; this is separate from whether real events can be forgotten. Do not call the 25% vivid, complete memories.

## Catarino et al. (2015) — voluntary suppression countercheck in PTSD

Catarino, A., Küpper, C. S., Werner-Seidler, A., Dalgleish, T., & Anderson, M. C. (2015). Failing to forget: Inhibitory-control deficits compromise memory suppression in posttraumatic stress disorder. *Psychological Science, 26*(5), 604–616. https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797615569889. [Publisher full article PDF](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/pdf/10.1177/0956797615569889); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25847536/). Published, peer-reviewed empirical experiment. **Full text read; methods/result verified** at pp. 607–612 (Participants, task, Fig. 3, Discussion).

18 people with PTSD and 18 trauma-exposed controls without PTSD learned object–aversive-scene pairs, then were explicitly instructed to retrieve or suppress the associated scene. Compared with controls, the PTSD group showed diminished suppression-induced forgetting across outcome measures; worse forgetting related to greater PTSD symptoms. This is a small, nonrandomized clinical comparison using newly learned material and deliberate suppression, not a test of unconscious blocking of an actual trauma. **Bearing:** retrieval inhibition is experimentally tractable but does not always become stronger after trauma; it offers a countercheck against inferring that overwhelming events are usually kept inaccessible for protection.

## Chu et al. (1999) — affirmative clinical reports, mechanism unresolved

Chu, J. A., Frey, L. M., Ganzel, B. L., & Matthews, J. A. (1999). Memories of childhood abuse: Dissociation, amnesia, and corroboration. *American Journal of Psychiatry, 156*(5), 749–755. https://doi.org/10.1176/ajp.156.5.749. [Publisher record](https://psychiatryonline.org/doi/10.1176/ajp.156.5.749); [complete public article copy](https://www.memoiretraumatique.org/assets/files/v1/Documents-pdf/1999-Am-J-Psy-Memories-of-Childhood-Abuse-Dissociation-Amnesia-and-Corroboration.pdf). Published empirical study. **Complete article read by Luna; Sol independently checked Table 6 and Discussion against full text.** Publisher PDF endpoint returned 403; public copy’s redistribution permission is unknown, so no copy was saved.

90 female inpatients in a trauma/dissociation unit completed abuse/dissociation measures and an interview; 90/148 approached enrolled (Methods, pp. 750–751). Abuse categories overlapped. Table 1 (p. 751): complete amnesia was reported by 20/75 physical-abuse, 25/74 sexual-abuse, and 13/64 witnessing groups; 38 **unique** women reported complete amnesia across types. Earlier abuse onset correlated with more reported amnesia for physical and sexual abuse (Table 2, p. 752). Of complete-amnesia cases who **attempted** corroboration, Table 6 (p. 754) shows verbal confirmation in 13/14 physical-abuse and 17/19 sexual-abuse cases; physical evidence was 5/14 and 5/19. The prose’s “more than half” physical-evidence description conflicts with Table 6; use the table and never label 89% physical/documentary verification. Retrospective event and amnesia reports, highly selected inpatients, overlap, and selective corroboration attempts limit inference. The authors themselves say the gaps may reflect broad processing/storage changes, **not necessarily repression** of overwhelming experiences (Discussion p. 753). **Bearing:** directly relevant affirmative evidence for reported gaps and some corroborated recovery, but no measured protective block or general-population “usually” rate.

## Hulbert & Anderson (2018) — strongest affirmative laboratory mechanism analogy

Hulbert, J. C., & Anderson, M. C. (2018). What doesn’t kill you makes you stronger: Psychological trauma and its relationship to enhanced memory control. *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, 147*(12), 1931–1949. https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0000461. [Cambridge repository full article](https://api.repository.cam.ac.uk/server/api/core/bitstreams/7e0cc5d7-8991-4cad-8b05-b65ca736d1f4/content); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30024184/). Published empirical two-experiment study. **Full author article read by Luna; Sol checked task description, integrated test, and experiment-2 caveat in full text** (article PDF pp. 2–3, 9–13, Figures 1–4; PDF has advance-online pagination).

Each experiment analyzed 48 undergraduates (24 higher, 24 lower self-reported childhood-trauma history; pooled N=96). Participants learned neutral cue–response word pairs with negative or neutral responses and were explicitly told to retrieve or suppress them on repeated Think/No-Think trials; final same-cue and independent-cue tests measured recall. In the integrated analysis of **both** experiments, the trauma-history × suppression-repetition interaction was significant, F(1,48)=7.48, p=.009; the higher-trauma group showed more suppression-induced forgetting across final tests (Results, PDF pp. 10–11, Fig. 3). The effect was collapsed across negative and neutral targets: valence moderation was nonsignificant, F(1,48)=1.21, p=.278; experiment moderation was F<1. The reported denominator 48 is an analysis degree of freedom, **not** the participant count or a negative-only subset; the article does not explain its exact construction (it matched trauma groups within 24 counterbalancing cells per experiment, PDF p. 4). Its continuous analysis explicitly identifies 96 participants (PDF p. 12). Experiment 2’s corresponding interaction alone was not significant, p=.122 (PDF p. 9). Trauma was self-reported, groups nonrandomized, and the task concerned **voluntary control of recently learned cued words**, not an involuntary missing autobiographical episode. **Bearing:** plausible retrieval-control capacity and a narrow trauma-history association; cannot establish the speaker’s automatic protective mechanism or its frequency. Read alongside Catarino et al. (2015), which found impaired deliberate suppression in PTSD.

## Dalenberg (1996) — small clinical corroboration comparison

Dalenberg, C. J. (1996). Accuracy, timing and circumstances of disclosure in therapy of recovered and continuous memories of abuse. *Journal of Psychiatry & Law, 24*(2), 229–275. https://doi.org/10.1177/009318539602400206. [Complete article](https://www.jimhopper.com/pdf/dalenberg1996.pdf); [publisher](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/009318539602400206). Published empirical clinical study. **Full text checked by Sol** at pp. 236–238, 246–249 (Tables 3–5), 255–260.

17 women, all former patients of the researcher, compared continuously remembered and later recovered abuse memory units against searched evidence; their living fathers participated. Table 4 (p. 248) reports similar proportions of evidence supporting memory units (74.6% continuous; 74.7% recovered), with roughly 29–32% of units lacking evidence. Seven fathers admitted some recovered-memory incidents (Discussion p. 255). Evidence categories included confessions and records but also contextual information; support for context or identity is not automatic proof of every alleged act. The therapist-investigator recruited a tiny selected group, and recovery histories were retrospective. The article itself describes some probable partly false/misattributed content (pp. 254–255, 260). **Bearing:** useful fair counterexample to blanket dismissal of recovered memories, not a protective-mechanism or prevalence test; reserve if Geraerts 2007 already covers corroboration more efficiently.

## Dalenberg et al. (2012) — affirmative synthesis, disputed causal interpretation

Dalenberg, C. J., Brand, B. L., Gleaves, D. H., Dorahy, M. J., Loewenstein, R. J., Cardeña, E., Frewen, P. A., Carlson, E. B., & Spiegel, D. (2012). Evaluation of the evidence for the trauma and fantasy models of dissociation. *Psychological Bulletin, 138*(3), 550–588. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0027447. [University-hosted full article](https://www.towson.edu/cla/departments/psychology/facultystaff/dalenbergetalevalevidencefortraumaandfantasymodelspsychbull2012.pdf). Published scholarly review/evidence synthesis, **not a new experiment**. **Full relevant text checked by Sol** at pp. 550, 565–567 (prediction 7/recovered-memory evidence), and conclusions.

Across eight predictions, the authors judge trauma–dissociation associations moderate and persistent after some controls, and interpret dissociation as a possible response to extreme emotion. They cite Williams (1995), Dalenberg (1996), and Geraerts (2007) as showing that some later memories are corroborated; they also note Geraerts’ 0/16 therapy group (p. 566–567). These cited cohorts are **not additional independent observations** simply because they appear in this review. The authors’ stronger causal conclusion concerns broad dissociation, which includes many experiences beyond a completely inaccessible event memory. Retrospective measures, mixed constructs, and causal interpretation are disputed by Lynn et al. (2014). **Bearing:** strongest course-linked affirmative framework; do not paraphrase it as a direct demonstration that the brain usually blocks uncertain trauma memories.

## Lynn et al. (2014) — counter-review of trauma model, not a new cohort

Lynn, S. J., Lilienfeld, S. O., Merckelbach, H., Giesbrecht, T., McNally, R. J., Loftus, E. F., Bruck, M., Garry, M., & Malaktaris, A. (2014). The trauma model of dissociation: Inconvenient truths and stubborn fictions. Comment on Dalenberg et al. (2012). *Psychological Bulletin, 140*(3), 896–910. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0035570. [Harvard open author article](https://dash.harvard.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/7312037d-d28e-6bd4-e053-0100007fdf3b/content); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24773505/). Published scholarly commentary with reanalysis, not an independent trauma-memory experiment. **Full relevant text checked by Sol** at author PDF pp. 1–3, 23–27 (Prediction 7), 48–49 (corroboration caveat).

The authors challenge causal inferences from trauma–dissociation correlations and the construct of encoded but inaccessible trauma memory. They explicitly allow trauma may cause some dissociation and that uncorroborated memories need not be false (author PDF endnote 6). Their structural-equation reanalysis uses existing correlational studies, so direction is still unresolved. Dalenberg et al.’s published reply (*Psychological Bulletin, 140*, 911–920; https://doi.org/10.1037/a0036685) is part of the dispute; its full text was **not** independently audited here. **Bearing:** mechanism and inference critique; useful paired with Dalenberg 2012, neither review settling a “usually” base rate.

## Anderson et al. (2025) — public beliefs and reported recovery, not mechanism

Anderson, K.-A. S., Miller, Q. C., Strange, D., & London, K. (2025). Repressed memories and the body keeps the score: Public perceptions and prevalence. *Memory, 33*(5), 495–509. https://doi.org/10.1080/09658211.2025.2479503. [Course full article](../../../course/week-06/6-2.pdf); [publisher](https://doi.org/10.1080/09658211.2025.2479503). Published empirical survey. **Complete local article available; Sol independently checked methods, Table 2, results, and discussion** at journal pp. 497–503 (course PDF pp. 4–10).

Online Prolific survey of 1,581 US adults, randomly assigned to direct abuse wording or descriptions of unwanted experiences. Follow-ups distinguished continuous memories, later reinterpretation, prior knowledge without memory, and no prior knowledge/memory. 57/1,581 (3.6%) self-reported the last category; 93.9% endorsed that traumatic memories can be repressed for years and recovered (journal pp. 499, 504; Table 2). The 3.6% is **a self-report of a recovery experience, not a validated prevalence of protective amnesia**, and question phrasing changed endorsement. The study did not expose people to this particular video, so its relationship and distress descriptions cannot prove the video’s impact. **Bearing:** audience beliefs and stakes; useful for explaining why causal certainty may matter to viewers while separately recognizing the speaker’s fair statement that symptoms can improve without memory recovery.

## Otgaar, Howe, & Patihis (2022) — concise skeptical review

Otgaar, H., Howe, M. L., & Patihis, L. (2022). What science tells us about false and repressed memories. *Memory, 30*(1), 16–21. https://doi.org/10.1080/09658211.2020.1870699. [Course full article](../../../course/week-06/6-3.pdf); [publisher](https://doi.org/10.1080/09658211.2020.1870699). Published narrative review, online first 2021, **not a new experiment**. **Sol checked original course PDF** at journal pp. 16–19 (course PDF pp. 2–5).

The authors identify alternative explanations for claims of recovered memory, including prior disclosures forgotten later, reinterpretation of remembered events, ordinary forgetting, and reluctance to discuss abuse (p. 18). They summarize variable false-memory implantation studies while acknowledging ethical/ecological limits: experimental negative events remain far from real child sexual abuse (pp. 17–18). They also note that intentional suppression experiments mostly use simple words/pictures and have replication concerns (p. 18). This is an argued overview; its “evidence lacking” conclusion cannot alone establish impossibility of protective amnesia or prevalence of any alternative. **Bearing:** map of alternative mechanisms and evidence boundaries; use primary studies for numerical claims.

## Goodman et al. (2003) — independently documented cohort, ABSTRACT-ONLY RESERVE

Goodman, G. S., Ghetti, S., Quas, J. A., Edelstein, R. S., Alexander, K. W., Redlich, A. D., Cordon, I. M., & Jones, D. P. H. (2003). A prospective study of memory for child sexual abuse: New findings relevant to the repressed-memory controversy. *Psychological Science, 14*(2), 113–118. https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-9280.01428. [Publisher record and abstract](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/psychological-science/1467-9280.01428/); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12661671/). Published empirical follow-up. **Primary abstract/metadata verified; full paper not accessed through the publisher login gate. Do not present unverified table/section detail as checked.**

The abstract reports 175 people with previously documented child sexual abuse histories and 81% reporting the abuse at follow-up; older age at abuse end, maternal support, and greater severity predicted disclosure. It explicitly cautions that failure to report does not necessarily mean an inaccessible memory, though true forgetting cannot be ruled out for a subset. A later coauthor synthesis gives cohort context, but those details were not independently verified in the 2003 original. **Bearing:** valuable documented-event comparator to Williams 1994 with a lower nonreport rate and explicit interpretive caution. Reserve until lawful full text is obtained if it will carry substantive claims beyond the abstract.

## ter Beek et al. (2026) — course preprint, EXCLUDE from published-science pool

ter Beek, M., Otgaar, H., Raymaekers, L., de Ruiter, C., Riesthuis, P., De Beuf, T., Hoving, A., Hogenkamp, M., & Verschuere, B. (2026). *The verifiability of recovered memories of abuse* [Unreviewed PsyArXiv preprint]. https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/4nt6p_v1. [Course full preprint](../../../course/week-06/6-1.pdf); [KU Leuven institutional listing](https://www.law.kuleuven.be/linc/english/staff/00134553/view?pubsonpage=20&pubtype=&sortby=scdate). **Status checked 29 September 2026:** course cover explicitly says it has not undergone peer review; KU Leuven still lists the item as a preprint, and no journal version was found in targeted title/DOI searches. This is evidence of current status, not proof a future version cannot appear.

The preprint analyzes 140 archival police statements classified by continuous, spontaneous, or active-search memory recovery. Its abstract and methods/results (course PDF pp. 1–2, 8–12, 19–22) report more verifiable details in continuous than active-search accounts; some exploratory comparisons of disproved details differ. The selected forensic sample and checkable-detail measure are not direct truth or base-rate tests. **Bearing:** research lead about recovery context only. The assignment requests published scientific literature; do not cite this preprint as a peer-reviewed essay source unless a journal publication is later verified.
