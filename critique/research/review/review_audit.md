## Claim-to-evidence check

| Evidence family | What it can establish | What it cannot establish |
|---|---|---|
| Documented-event follow-up (Williams 1994/1995; Goodman 2003 abstract) | Some documented abuse events are not later reported; some later recollections match earlier records. | That nonreport equals inaccessible encoded memory, or that protective blocking is its usual cause. |
| Clinical amnesia/corroboration (Chu 1999; Dalenberg 1996) | Selected patients report gaps; some later reports have corroborating evidence. | Population prevalence, mechanism, or validity of every remembered detail. |
| Recovery-context comparisons (Geraerts 2007/2009) | Spontaneous versus active/suggestive contexts differ on corroboration and lab cognitive profiles. | Randomized causal effect of therapy; actual truth of every uncorroborated report; a universal explanation for delayed recall. |
| Suppression experiments (Hulbert & Anderson 2018; Catarino 2015) | Explicit retrieval control of newly learned material can affect later recall; trauma-history and PTSD groups differ in these tasks. | Automatic protective blocking of a whole autobiographical trauma, or how often it happens in uncertain viewers. |
| Suggestive false-memory experiment (Loftus & Pickrell 1995) | Deliberate, family-backed suggestion can produce some full/partial autobiographical false reports. | That abuse memories are usually false or that the speaker prescribed this procedure. |
| Reviews and public beliefs (Dalenberg 2012; Lynn 2014; Otgaar 2022; Anderson 2025) | Competing interpretations and reasons public wording matters. | New independent mechanism data; causal effects of this particular video on viewers. |

The decisive missing link is a design that **identifies causes of memory gaps and their frequencies in the population addressed by “usually.”** None of these candidates supplies that denominator and differential-cause measure. This is an inference from study design, not proof that protective amnesia never occurs.

## Corrections and overstatement controls from original-source checks

- **Williams 1994:** Raw note overemphasized childhood amnesia. The article states most participants were at least 7 at abuse, and 28% of those aged 7+ did not recall the index event (p. 1171, Table 1). Younger age remains relevant, but cannot explain all nonreport. The headline 38% is nonreport at a later interview, not measured repression.
- **Williams 1994/1995:** Same 129-woman cohort and interview, not two independent replications. The correct 1995 publisher DOI is `10.1002/jts.2490080408`; some older reference lists use a Springer-era DOI alias.
- **Chu 1999:** The amnesia-group denominators by abuse type overlap; 20+25+13 must not be treated as 58 unique women. Table 6 reports verbal confirmations in 13/14 physical-abuse and 17/19 sexual-abuse attempts, with physical evidence in only 5/14 and 5/19. The prose statement that “more than half” found physical evidence conflicts with those table cells; retain the table values and flag the discrepancy. The authors explicitly say broad autobiographical gaps may reflect processing/storage changes rather than repression (p. 753).
- **Geraerts 2007:** The 0/16 in-therapy corroboration cell is not a count of proved false memories. The authors acknowledge that absence of corroboration is not disproof and that therapy context was observational, not assigned. The full title includes “Corroborating continuous and discontinuous memories of childhood sexual abuse.”
- **Geraerts 2009:** The therapy-recovery group specifically reported *suggestive* methods; its DRM outcome is a false-word-list measure, not evidence that the actual abuse memory was false. Spontaneous-recovery participants’ greater “forgot-it-all-along” task effect is an alternative mechanism marker, not direct proof of what happened earlier in their lives.
- **Loftus & Pickrell 1995:** Six of 24 (25%) gave full **or partial** lost-in-mall reports, including speculation; their false reports were generally less detailed and clear than true ones. We read an author manuscript close to final publication, so precise typeset wording and omitted figures were not checked.
- **Hulbert & Anderson 2018:** The pooled trauma-history × suppression interaction is significant (`p=.009`), while the corresponding Experiment 2 interaction alone is not (`p=.122`). The task uses explicit voluntary suppression of new word pairs; no inference of involuntary trauma memory blockade is warranted. Self-reported trauma and nonrandom groups preclude causal attribution of better lab control to trauma history.
- **Catarino 2015:** The PTSD group showed *less* suppression-induced forgetting than trauma-exposed controls. This is a relevant countercheck, but PTSD intrusive memory symptoms and small N=36 limit generalization to people with an uncertain gap.
- **Dalenberg 2012 versus Lynn 2014:** Both are published scholarly syntheses with opposing causal interpretations of largely overlapping literatures. They are not two independent datasets. Lynn’s critique explicitly allows trauma may cause some dissociation and that uncorroborated memories need not be false; Dalenberg’s broad dissociation conclusion should not be recast as a measured “usually” rate for whole-event amnesia. Dalenberg et al.’s 2014 reply is acknowledged, not full-text audited here.
- **Anderson 2025:** 3.6% is self-reported no-prior-knowledge/no-prior-memory *recovery claims*, not verified trauma or a mechanism prevalence. The belief item showed 93.9% agreement with long-term repression/recovery; these respondents were not experimentally exposed to the assigned video. The course abstract’s 94% is a rounding.
- **Otgaar 2022:** A concise, argued narrative review; its approximately 30% false-memory figure is drawn from other experiments with varied criteria. Its own text flags ecological limits and that suppression studies typically use simple material. Use a primary study for a numerical laboratory example.

## Screening decisions and fair coverage

The fifteen reviewed records span genuine memory gaps and later corroboration; trauma-linked dissociation; experimental retrieval control in both supportive and contrary directions; ordinary/reconstructive alternatives; false recollection under suggestion; and audience beliefs. This prevents a one-sided claim that all recovered memories are false, and also prevents treating every gap as the same protective phenomenon. The speaker’s caveat that memories may never return and symptoms can improve anyway is supported as a fair reading of the transcript; no source here tests EMDR efficacy or justifies expanding into that topic.

Three first-pass leads were **screened out of the 15-record review set**: Chiu et al. (2012), `10.1016/j.psychres.2011.10.002`, because the nonclinical recovered-memory/dissociation survey is self-report, full text was not accessed, and its distinct contribution is limited once stronger primary studies are present; Staniloiu, Markowitsch, & Kordon (2018), `10.1016/j.neuropsychologia.2017.10.017`, because a selected 28-case psychogenic-amnesia series has no population denominator and the explicit “protective” function is an author interpretation, while full text remained gated; and Dorahy et al. (2014), `10.1177/0004867414527523`, because its DID overview is more remote from an ordinary uncertain-event memory gap than the retained trauma/dissociation review. These remain searchable leads, not disproof of their findings.

**Goodman 2003** is a valuable prospective comparator but is an **abstract-only reserve** until the original full text can be obtained through the university library. **Dalenberg 1996** and **Williams 1995** are full-text checked yet likely reserves in a 750–1,000-word essay because the former has a tiny selected therapist sample and the latter duplicates Williams 1994’s cohort. **Loftus & Pickrell 1995** is optional for a brief accuracy caution; it should not dominate a critique of the video's causal claim. **ter Beek 2026** must be excluded from the assignment’s published-science references unless a peer-reviewed version is verified later.
