# Course-lead supplement: Anderson et al. (2025), Otgaar et al. (2022), Goodman et al. (2003)

**Status: UNREVIEWED supplement.** This is separate from the reviewed evidence set. It adds the specific course-supplied readings and Goodman et al. requested for the memory-claim research. It does not change the essay, checklist, plan, or evidence table. The bounded question remains whether inability to remember a possible traumatic event is *usually caused by protective blocking*. Distinguish a reported gap, its proposed cause, and the accuracy of a later memory.

## Anderson et al. (2025): public beliefs and self-reported recovery claims

Kris-Ann S. Anderson, Quincy C. Miller, Deryn Strange, & Kamala London. (2025). Repressed memories and the body keeps the score: Public perceptions and prevalence. *Memory, 33*(5), 495–509. https://doi.org/10.1080/09658211.2025.2479503. Peer-reviewed empirical survey; published online 23 March 2025; issue publication 2025. Course copy: [`course/week-06/6-2.pdf`](../../../../course/week-06/6-2.pdf). Publisher record/full-text route: [Taylor & Francis](https://doi.org/10.1080/09658211.2025.2479503). Open materials/data reported by article at OSF: https://osf.io/w7k9v/.

**Design/sample.** Nonclinical online survey of US adults recruited via Prolific, described as nationally representative. 1,634 recruited in April–May 2022; after incomplete surveys were excluded, N=1,581 (mean age 44.09, SD 15.99). Random assignment to either a direct child-abuse question or an “unwanted experiences” question set, with follow-up clarification to distinguish continuous memories, reinterpretation, prior knowledge but no memory, and no prior knowledge/memory. Self-report only; participants could skip sensitive questions. PDF locators: PDF pp. 4–5 (journal pp. 497–498) for participants/procedure and coding.

**Relevant results.** Of N=1,581, 3.6% (n=57) reported recovering memories for abuse of which they had no prior knowledge or memory. Depending on the question format, 18.6% of the “yes” subgroup under direct questioning (21/113) and 4.8% of the unwanted-experience subgroup (23/480) fell in this no-prior-knowledge/memory category; the paper reports 7.2% (n=114) as a broader category including people who knew of abuse but said they had no memory. The authors emphasize that direct questioning elicited more “recovered-memory” claims, whereas 39.2% of respondents in the unwanted-experience condition said they later reinterpreted remembered experiences as abuse. Among the 57 no-prior-memory/knowledge claimants, mean confidence in accuracy was 74% (abstract rounds to 75%); only 6 (10.5%) said they first remembered in therapy. Among 51 asked about the context outside therapy, 40% (n=20) described external suggestions/triggers in discussion with family/friends/media; this is retrospective participant report, not independent verification of trigger or event. Locators: PDF pp. 5–8 (journal pp. 498–501), especially Table 2 on PDF p. 6/journal p. 499 and recovery details PDF pp. 7–8/journal pp. 500–501. Consequences reported include broken contact and relationship strain; PDF p. 9–10/journal pp. 502–503.

**Limits and fit.** Useful for audience context: claims are not vanishingly rare, definitions/question phrasing strongly affect reports, public belief in repression is common, and reported recovery can affect confidence/relationships. It does not independently verify abuse or memory accuracy, cannot distinguish genuine event forgetting from reinterpretation, source confusion, ordinary forgetting, or false recollection, and does not establish a causal mechanism or population frequency for protective blocking. Prolific/online panel and self-report/sensitive-item nonresponse limit prevalence estimates; the 57-person subgroup is small. Avoid saying this survey demonstrates either that memories were true or that they were false. It shows how an audience may parse the speaker’s simple causal explanation and why the “usually” inference is not tested.

## Otgaar, Howe, & Patihis (2022): review of false and repressed memory research

Henry Otgaar, Mark L. Howe, & Lawrence Patihis. (2022). What science tells us about false and repressed memories. *Memory, 30*(1), 16–21. https://doi.org/10.1080/09658211.2020.1870699. Review article (not new empirical study). Published online 12 January 2021; issue citation is 2022. Course copy: [`course/week-06/6-3.pdf`](../../../../course/week-06/6-3.pdf). Publisher version: [Taylor & Francis](https://doi.org/10.1080/09658211.2020.1870699).

**Content and locators.** Concise narrative overview of false-memory implantation, possible negative/repeated false events, evidence and alternative explanations concerning “repressed” memories, and public beliefs. Abstract: PDF p. 2/journal p. 16. False-memory paradigms, variable yields (reported across studies as 0–70%), and ethical/ecological limits on implanting severe events: PDF pp. 3–4/journal pp. 17–18. Alternative accounts of forgetting claims include reluctance/avoidance, “forgot-it-all-along” (forgetting having disclosed or remembered earlier), later reinterpretation, and normal retrieval/forgetting; review states traumatic experiences are generally remembered well: PDF p. 4/journal p. 18. Belief surveys and concluding position that evidence for unconscious repression is lacking and existing suppression work is limited/not ecologically matched to autobiographical trauma: PDF p. 5/journal p. 19. The review’s closing “features” box is at PDF p. 5/journal p. 19. The reference list begins PDF pp. 6–7/journal pp. 20–21.

**Limitations and fit.** This is a short, explicitly argumentative overview, not a systematic review/meta-analysis and not an experiment. It provides a compact route to primary literature and clarifies that laboratory false-memory results do not prove false abuse memories are common. The authors themselves acknowledge that implant studies’ negative events remain a far step from real abuse (journal p. 18), and suppression experiments largely use simple word/picture material with replication concerns (journal p. 18). Treat its verdict as one scholarly synthesis in a disputed literature; do not use the review alone to prove absence of protective amnesia or to collapse all retrieval inhibition into Freud’s unconscious repression construct. It qualifies mechanism claims and later-accuracy certainty, while leaving room for real memory gaps and true recoveries in some cases.

## Goodman et al. (2003): prospective follow-up of documented CSA

Gail S. Goodman, Simona Ghetti, Jodi A. Quas, Robin S. Edelstein, Kristen Weede Alexander, Allison D. Redlich, Ingrid M. Cordon, & David P. H. Jones. (2003). A prospective study of memory for child sexual abuse: New findings relevant to the repressed-memory controversy. *Psychological Science, 14*(2), 113–118. https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-9280.01428. Peer-reviewed empirical article. Primary record/abstract: [Association for Psychological Science](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/psychological-science/1467-9280.01428/); PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12661671/; bibliographic/institution record: [CSU Sacramento repository](https://scholars.csus.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/A-Prospective-Study-of-Memory-for/99257876763201671).

**Design/sample/method.** Prospective follow-up of people with documented childhood sexual abuse histories; 175 individuals were interviewed as adults/older adolescents about their childhood abuse. A later coauthor review describes the original cohort as 4–17-year-olds involved as alleged victims in prosecutions, with police/prosecutor records and prior child interviews/observations, and follow-up about 14 years after legal involvement (up to 21 years after assault); see Goodman, Quas, Goldfarb, Gonzalves, & Gonzalez (2019), “Trauma and Long-Term Memory for Childhood Events: Impact Matters,” *Child Development Perspectives, 13*(1), 3–9, https://doi.org/10.1111/cdep.12307, section “Longitudinal Studies of Child Maltreatment and Memory.” The 2003 primary article abstract reports 175 participants and the recall outcome; article pages 113–118, abstract and method/results sections are the relevant locators. Primary full text appears to be behind APS/publisher login: APS article page states “Log in … to read the full article.” A lawful open copy was not located in this search. I did not rely on third-party upload copies. An independent contemporaneous methodological discussion is Ross E. Cheit (2003), “The Limitations of a Prospective Study of Memories for Child Sexual Abuse,” *Journal of Child Sexual Abuse, 12*(2), 105–111, DOI https://doi.org/10.1300/J070v12n02_06; [ERIC abstract](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ843712).

**Results.** 81% of 175 interviewees reported the documented abuse, leaving 19% who did not report the documented event in the follow-up interview. Greater age when abuse ended, maternal support after disclosure, and greater abuse severity were associated with increased disclosure; ethnicity and dissociation also played a role. The authors explicitly state that failure to report should not necessarily be interpreted as evidence the abuse was inaccessible to memory, though forgetting/inaccessibility cannot be ruled out for a subset. Precise locator: APS abstract; journal pp. 113–118 for full report. Another coauthor synthesis notes some subgroups had non-disclosure as high as 30% (Goodman et al., 2019), underscoring that overall average should not erase heterogeneity.

**Limitations and fit.** This strong design improves on retrospective abuse histories because the events were documented before the adult follow-up. Yet nonreport is not proof of amnesia: participants may not disclose, may interpret prompts differently, or may fail to match the target event. The prosecution-based documented cohort is a selected sample and cannot establish a base rate among all trauma survivors or explain why any one participant did not report. The original cohort involved children with previous case/prosecution contact, potentially shaping later memory and disclosure; follow-up participation/selection also limits generalization. Prospective documentation tests nonreport more strongly than protective blocking: no measure directly shows an encoded memory became inaccessible through a protective mechanism. This source is a key qualification because it documents that most did report the abuse and warns against treating nondisclosure as proof of inaccessibility, while allowing that genuine forgetting may occur in some cases.

## Relevance to the bounded claim

Taken together, these sources support calibrated language. Some people report long gaps and some such reports concern independently documented events; later nonreport can occur. None of the three establishes that the usual cause is a nervous-system protective block. Goodman et al. directly warns against equating nonreport with inaccessibility. Anderson et al. measures contemporary self-reports and beliefs, not event truth or mechanism. Otgaar et al. is a contested scholarly overview that explains plausible alternatives and laboratory boundaries. The speaker’s acknowledgement that memories may never return and symptoms may improve without them can be recognized without endorsing the frequency/cause assertion.
