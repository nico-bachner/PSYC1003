# Contrary and qualifying evidence: memory gaps, mechanism, and later accuracy

**Status: UNREVIEWED source-gathering memo; not an essay verdict.** Prepared for the bounded question: is inability to remember a possible traumatic event *usually* due to protective blocking? Keep these propositions separate: (1) some people report gaps in memory for documented abuse; (2) those gaps were caused by a protective inability to retrieve a stored memory; and (3) later recovered memories are accurate. Evidence for one proposition does not establish the others.

The speaker fairly says memories may or may not return and symptoms can improve without recovery. The transcript does not promise accurate recovery or explicitly recommend suggestion techniques. This memo therefore does not infer that the video itself gives recovery instructions. It does not evaluate EMDR efficacy.

## Candidate sources

### 1. Williams (1994) — prospective documented-event evidence; qualifying

Linda M. Williams, “Recall of Childhood Trauma: A Prospective Study of Women’s Memories of Child Sexual Abuse,” *Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology, 62*(6), 1167–1176. DOI: [10.1037/0022-006X.62.6.1167](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-006X.62.6.1167). Empirical, peer-reviewed. Full text available at [author-posted/research-host PDF](https://www.memoiretraumatique.org/assets/files/v1/Documents-pdf/1994-Anglais-Souvenir-du-traumatisme-de-l-enfance-Linda-M-Williams.pdf); abstract also at [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7860814/).

Design/sample: 129 adult women with documented childhood sexual victimization were re-interviewed about 17 years after a hospital report. The initial report is an important independent record, but recall and interview responses remained retrospective. Results: 38% did not report the abuse event during the follow-up interview; younger age at abuse and perpetrator familiarity predicted nonrecall. Locator: abstract; article pp. 1167–1176, especially discussion at p. 1174 (interpret cautiously: forgetting does not itself establish repression/protective blocking). The study supports the existence of later nonrecall for documented events; it cannot identify the mechanism, distinguish ordinary childhood forgetting from motivated avoidance or dissociative amnesia, or establish that this is usually why any given person has a gap. Many abuse reports were based on events at very young ages, making childhood amnesia a material alternative. Do not overstate the headline 38% as 38% “repressed.”

### 2. Williams (1995) — prospective cohort and corroboration; qualifying

Linda M. Williams, “Recovered Memories of Abuse in Women with Documented Child Sexual Victimization Histories,” *Journal of Traumatic Stress, 8*(4), 649–673. DOI: [10.1002/jts.2490080408](https://doi.org/10.1002/jts.2490080408). Empirical, peer-reviewed. Publisher record/abstract: [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/jts.2490080408).

Design/sample: follow-up of the same 129 women with documented histories, 17 years after original report. Of 80 who recalled the abuse, 10% (16% of those recalling) said they had at some point forgotten it. The accounts of women reporting a forgetting period and those continuously remembering had the same number of discrepancies against early-1970s reports. Locator: abstract; article pp. 649–673. Supports that at least some reports of recovered memory can concern real events and that reported discontinuity does not automatically mean falsehood. Small recovered-memory subgroup; same cohort as Williams 1994, so not independent replication. Self-reported past forgetting and comparison to initial records cannot show a protective mechanism, and a count of discrepancies is not a comprehensive truth test.

### 3. Chu, Frey, Ganzel, & Matthews (1999) — clinical amnesia and corroboration; affirmative/qualifying

James A. Chu, Lisa M. Frey, Barbara L. Ganzel, & Julia A. Matthews, “Memories of Childhood Abuse: Dissociation, Amnesia, and Corroboration,” *American Journal of Psychiatry, 156*(5), 749–755. DOI: [10.1176/ajp.156.5.749](https://doi.org/10.1176/ajp.156.5.749). Empirical, peer-reviewed. Full text at [publisher PDF](https://psychiatryonline.org/doi/pdf/10.1176/ajp.156.5.749) and abstract at [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10327909/).

Design/sample: 90 female patients at a specialist trauma-related-disorders unit; self-report measures and structured interview covered abuse, dissociation, memory gaps, recovery circumstances, suggestion, and corroboration. Abuse reporters had elevated dissociation; earlier onset/chronicity related to higher symptom scores and greater reported amnesia. Among participants with complete amnesia who tried to corroborate, corroboration was common; table 6 (journal p. 754) reports attempted corroboration and physical/verbal support by abuse type; authors summarize 89% corroboration among the sexual-abuse subgroup attempting confirmation at p. 754. Most said memories first returned at home/alone or with family/friends; few were in session (results pp. 752–754). Supports that reported amnesia and corroborated recovered abuse memories occur in some clinical patients. Severe referral/selection bias, retrospective reports, subjective/subtle suggestion may not be detected, and family/verbal confirmations differ in evidentiary strength. Does not test whether protective blocking caused amnesia, nor the population frequency implied by “usually.”

### 4. Dalenberg (1996) — clinical corroboration comparison; qualifying

Constance J. Dalenberg, “Accuracy, Timing and Circumstances of Disclosure in Therapy of Recovered and Continuous Memories of Abuse,” *Journal of Psychiatry & Law, 24*(2), 229–275. DOI: [10.1177/009318539602400206](https://doi.org/10.1177/009318539602400206). Empirical, peer-reviewed. Author-hosted copy indexed at [PDF](https://www.jimhopper.com/pdf/dalenberg1996.pdf); publisher abstract at [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/009318539602400206).

Design/sample: 17 psychotherapy patients who reported recovered abuse memories participated in a search for evidence confirming/refuting memories; comparison was with continuous memories/details and evidence ratings. Abstract reports recovered memories were as accurate as continuous memories by the study’s evidence criteria; predictors of corroborated memory units included memory/perceptual accuracy measures. Locator: abstract; article pp. 229–275 (consult full text for operational criteria before citing specific rates). Important counterweight: memory discontinuity alone does not render a report false. Limitations: very small, selected sample; evidence search and ratings do not establish event-level ground truth in every case; not a test of why the earlier gap occurred or how prevalent the mechanism is.

### 5. Geraerts et al. (2007) — corroboration differs by recovery context; qualifying

Elke Geraerts, Jonathan W. Schooler, Harald Merckelbach, Marko Jelicic, Beatrijs J. A. Hauer, & Zara Ambadar, “The Reality of Recovered Memories,” *Psychological Science, 18*(7), 564–568. DOI: [10.1111/j.1467-9280.2007.01940.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2007.01940.x). Empirical, peer-reviewed. Publisher/author abstract: [APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/psychological-science/j.1467-9280.2007.01940.x); full text accessible via [author-hosted PDF](https://sites.pitt.edu/~bertsch/Geraerts.pdf).

Design/sample: participants reporting childhood sexual abuse were interviewed; two independent raters searched for corroborating information. Continuous and discontinuous memories recalled unexpectedly outside therapy were more likely to be corroborated than memories recovered in therapy after anticipation/effort. Outside-therapy memories were also associated with greater surprise. Locator: article pp. 564–568, abstract and study results. Qualifies both “recovered memories are false” and “recovery proves protective blocking”: the route of retrieval matters, and later accuracy is mixed. Sample was self-selected and corroboration is not a perfect ground-truth standard; absence of confirmation does not prove falsehood. It does not estimate all population memory gaps.

### 6. Geraerts et al. (2009) — cognitive mechanisms vary by subgroup; qualifying

Elke Geraerts, D. Stephen Lindsay, Harald Merckelbach, Marko Jelicic, Linsey Raymaekers, Michelle M. Arnold, & Jonathan W. Schooler, “Cognitive Mechanisms Underlying Recovered-Memory Experiences of Childhood Sexual Abuse,” *Psychological Science, 20*(1), 92–98. DOI: [10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02247.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02247.x). Empirical, peer-reviewed. Abstract at [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19037903/) and [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02247.x); openly accessible copy: [PDF](https://edkardas.com/Courses/DPASU/geraerts-et-al-2009-cognitive-mechanisms-underlying-recovered-memory-experiences-of-childhood-sexual-abuse.pdf).

Design/sample: compared groups reporting continuously remembered CSA, spontaneously recovered memories, suggestive-therapy recovered memories, and controls with cognitive tasks measuring susceptibility to false memories and “forgot-it-all-along” errors. The therapy-recovery subgroup showed increased susceptibility to false-memory construction but not increased underestimation of prior remembering; spontaneous-recovery subgroup showed the converse pattern. Locator: abstract; article pp. 92–98. This supports heterogeneous processes and cautions against treating a gap as one universal protective process. Group definitions and retrospective classifications, modest specialized samples, and tasks are not direct diagnoses of event truth or causal mechanisms.

### 7. Loftus & Pickrell (1995) — laboratory false autobiographical memory; accuracy qualifier

Elizabeth F. Loftus & Jacqueline E. Pickrell, “The Formation of False Memories,” *Psychiatric Annals, 25*(12), 720–725. DOI: [10.3928/0048-5713-19951201-07](https://doi.org/10.3928/0048-5713-19951201-07). Empirical, peer-reviewed. Publisher metadata/abstract landing: [Healio](https://journals.healio.com/doi/10.3928/0048-5713-19951201-07); author explanation: [University of Washington](https://staff.washington.edu/eloftus/Articles/sciam.htm).

Design/sample: 24 adults received four childhood-event narratives, one false “lost in a shopping mall” event, with a family member’s assistance; interviewed repeatedly. About 25% reported some memory for the false event (reported in paper, pp. 720–725). Demonstrates that suggestion can produce some false autobiographical recollection, so later emergence alone cannot verify accuracy. Does not demonstrate that abuse memories are usually false, that trauma gaps reflect this mechanism, or that ordinary trauma-informed therapy is equivalent to this deliberate implantation protocol. Mundane suggested event and family-collusion procedure limit generalization to traumatic events.

### 8. Lynn et al. (2014) — critical review/comment; conceptual qualifier, not an experiment

Steven J. Lynn, Scott O. Lilienfeld, Harald Merckelbach, Timo Giesbrecht, Richard J. McNally, Elizabeth F. Loftus, et al., “The Trauma Model of Dissociation: Inconvenient Truths and Stubborn Fictions. Comment on Dalenberg et al. (2012),” *Psychological Bulletin, 140*(3), 896–910. DOI: [10.1037/a0035570](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0035570). Published peer-reviewed scholarly commentary/review, not new empirical research. Abstract and bibliographic record: [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24335599/); open author manuscript: [PDF](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/261953090_The_Trauma_Model_of_Dissociation_Inconvenient_Truths_and_Stubborn_Fictions_Comment_on).

This critique responds to Dalenberg et al.’s 2012 review/meta-analysis of trauma and dissociation. The authors argue that trauma-dissociation correlations do not directly prove a specific protective amnesia mechanism, and point to methodological concerns including self-report, causal inference, and whether alleged amnesia was independently established. Locator: pp. 896–910, especially p. 905 for the claim that studies of corroborated traumatic events had not provided convincing evidence of dissociative amnesia in the strong sense of encoded trauma rendered inaccessible. This is a theoretical synthesis/commentary within an active scholarly dispute, not an independent dataset and not a final consensus statement. Pair it with the reply: Dalenberg et al. (2014), “Reality versus fantasy: A reply to Lynn et al. (2014),” *Psychological Bulletin, 140*(3), 911–920, DOI [10.1037/a0036685](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0036685), which argues that trauma is a causal risk factor for dissociation and defends the prior meta-analysis. Use the exchange to establish dispute and construct validity limits, not to imply that either view by itself answers the video’s “usually” claim.

### 9. ter Beek et al. (2026) — archival statement verifiability; PREPRINT ONLY, pending status review

Mirjam ter Beek, Henry Otgaar, Linsey Raymaekers, Corine de Ruiter, Paul Riesthuis, Tamara De Beuf, Anne Hoving, Meike Hogenkamp, & Bruno Verschuere, “The Verifiability of Recovered Memories of Abuse.” PsyArXiv preprint, DOI [10.31234/osf.io/4nt6p_v1](https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/4nt6p_v1), deposited June 2026. **Current publication-status check (2026-09-29): still identified as “preprint” by KU Leuven’s institutional research record; no peer-reviewed journal/version-of-record record was found in publisher or bibliographic searches.** [KU Leuven/LINC author repository record](https://www.law.kuleuven.be/linc/english/staff/00134553/view?pubsonpage=20&pubtype=&sortby=scdate) identifies the 2026 item as “preprint”; [ResearchGate record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/407006938_The_Verifiability_of_Recovered_Memories_of_Abuse) also labels it a preprint and indicates no peer review. The course copy is [week 6, 6-1.pdf](../../../../course/week-06/6-1.pdf), whose cover itself says “unreviewed preprint.” Do not cite it as published or peer-reviewed. DOI resolver/API inaccessible in the web tool; status conclusion rests on the primary institutional repository plus PsyArXiv/preprint metadata, not an absolute proof of nonpublication. Recheck before final essay.

Design/sample/results from preprint: archival police statements/case files from 140 delayed child-sexual-abuse reports (86% female; mean reported onset age 6.6; mean report delay 12.4 years); memory type coded continuous, spontaneous recovery, or recovery after active search. Detailed locators in supplied 31-page PDF: methods/sample roughly pp. 8–12; results table/analysis around pp. 19–22. Confirmatory results: continuous-memory statements contained more verifiable details than active-search recovered statements. Exploratory analyses found details in active-search statements were more often disproved; strong Bayesian evidence for active-search vs continuous in determined outcomes, but spontaneous-recovered comparisons were small/uncertain. Supports caution about accuracy of active-search accounts; cannot establish truth/falsity of all recovered memories or a general cognitive cause for memory gaps. Forensic archival sample is highly selected (reported cases under investigation); verifiability/disconfirmation is not the same as event truth and results are not general-population prevalence estimates.

## Cross-cutting interpretation to retain

- Williams (1994), Chu et al. (1999), Dalenberg (1996), and Geraerts et al. (2007) make a simplistic denial of real memory gaps or all recovered memories untenable. They do **not** demonstrate that a brain protection mechanism is the usual cause.
- A prospective file plus later nonreport is stronger evidence of nonrecall than a retrospective claim alone, yet still cannot discriminate childhood amnesia, ordinary forgetting, retrieval cue changes, deliberate avoidance, reinterpretation, source confusion, or a specific dissociative block.
- Retrospective surveys and clinical samples cannot establish how often protective blocking is the causal mechanism in uncertain individuals. “Usually” needs direct base-rate and causal evidence, not just existence evidence.
- Geraerts et al. and the unreviewed ter Beek preprint distinguish spontaneous recovery from active search; avoid conflating these contexts or claiming the speaker prescribed the latter.
- False-memory paradigms show a boundary condition on accuracy, not that real abuse commonly goes unremembered or that traumatic memory reports are generally false. Treat source status and ecological limits plainly.
