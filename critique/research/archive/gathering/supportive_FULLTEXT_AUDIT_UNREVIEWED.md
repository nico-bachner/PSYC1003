## Chu et al. (1999): full text read; exact denominators and results

**Reference:** Chu, J. A., Frey, L. M., Ganzel, B. L., & Matthews, J. A. (1999). Memories of childhood abuse: Dissociation, amnesia, and corroboration. *American Journal of Psychiatry, 156*(5), 749–755. https://doi.org/10.1176/ajp.156.5.749. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10327909/) · [publisher PDF](https://psychiatryonline.org/doi/pdf/10.1176/ajp.156.5.749).

**Full-text access status:** The direct publisher PDF endpoint returned HTTP 403 in this web reader. I read the complete publisher-formatted seven-page article PDF, as indexed and hosted at [this public mirror](https://www.memoiretraumatique.org/assets/files/v1/Documents-pdf/1999-Am-J-Psy-Memories-of-Childhood-Abuse-Dissociation-Amnesia-and-Corroboration.pdf). I did not save a local copy. The mirror's permission status is not independently known, so prefer the publisher/University library copy before downloading or redistributing it. The data below are from the article itself, not its abstract alone.

**Sample and measure:** 90 female psychiatric inpatients (ages 18–60) from a unit specializing in posttraumatic and dissociative disorders; of 179 meeting inclusion criteria, 31 discharged before recruitment, 148 were approached, and 90 enrolled (article Methods, pp. 750–751). Participants completed abuse-history and Dissociative Experiences Scale measures plus a structured interview about amnesia, first recall, suggestion, and corroboration.

**Amnesia counts and exact evidence:** In the full sample, 75/90 (83%) reported physical abuse, 74/90 (82%) sexual abuse, and 64/90 (71%) witnessing violence (Results, p. 751). Table 1 (p. 751; PDF page 3) reports amnesia categories among each abuse group:

- Physical abuse: 31/75 reported no amnesia, 24/75 partial amnesia, and 20/75 complete amnesia (58.7% partial or complete; 26.7% complete).
- Sexual abuse: 30/74 no, 19/74 partial, and 25/74 complete (59.5% partial or complete; 33.8% complete).
- Witnessing abuse: 34/64 no, 17/64 partial, and 13/64 complete (46.9% partial or complete; 20.3% complete).

These are subgroup prevalences inside a highly selected inpatient sample, not population estimates. Table 1 and text also report dissociation-score differences across no/partial/complete amnesia: physical-abuse groups χ²(2)=11.50, p=.003; sexual-abuse groups χ²(2)=9.18, p=.01; witnessing-abuse groups χ²(2)=19.15, p=.0001 (Results, p. 751). Earlier abuse onset correlated with higher reported amnesia for physical abuse (Spearman r=−.39, N=73, p=.001) and sexual abuse (r=−.55, N=73, p<.001), but not witnessed abuse (r=−.08, N=62, p=.55; Results, pp. 751–752; Table 2, p. 752).

**Recovered-memory and corroboration denominators:** The authors summarize 38 women as reporting complete amnesia; these women can appear in more than one abuse category, so the Table 4–6 column Ns (physical 20, sexual 25, witnessed 13) are overlapping event-type groups and sum to 58, not 58 unique participants. Table 4 (p. 752) indicates first recollection was often at home and alone; only 1 physical-abuse and 2 sexual-abuse cases reported first recall in a therapy session (and 2 for witnessed abuse). Table 5 (p. 754): among complete-amnesia cases, overt suggestion was denied by 17/20 physical, 21/25 sexual, and 11/13 witness categories; “don’t know” responses were 1, 0, and 2 respectively.

Table 6 (p. 754) and Results (p. 753) report that many complete-amnesia cases attempted corroboration; among those who sought confirmation of sexual abuse, verbal confirmation was found in 17/19 (89%); for physical abuse, 13/14 (93%); and for witnessing abuse 3/6 (50%). Physical evidence was found by 5/14 physical-abuse and 5/19 sexual-abuse cases attempting corroboration (Table 6). These denominators are very small and reflect participants who chose/managed to seek corroboration. In Discussion (p. 754), the authors explicitly note some may have been validating abuse they had always remembered, despite being asked about recovered memories.

**Critical mechanism distinction from the full text:** This paper supports reported amnesia associated with abuse and dissociation in a treatment-seeking subgroup, plus some independently corroborated later reports. It does **not** demonstrate that memories were protectively blocked. In fact, for patients describing gaps across whole periods of life, the authors say (Discussion, p. 753) the proposed mechanism “may not be repression of overwhelming experiences or selective inattention to noxious events”; they instead suggest chronic trauma may change processing and storage across entire autobiographical periods. This is a proposed interpretation, not a measured causal mechanism. They also acknowledge some participants reporting substantial abuse had little dissociation/amnesia (p. 753).

**Authors’ limitations (pp. 754–755):** Retrospective self-reports about abuse, amnesia, and recovery may contain distortions (including errors distinguishing degrees of amnesia and remembering when/how recall occurred); sample was primarily Caucasian women and predominantly disabled patients receiving trauma treatment; multiple abuse types overlapped; study did not examine severe neglect; and its inpatient cross-sectional design does not identify mechanisms or explain how some traumatized people avoid dissociative/PTSD symptoms. Authors conclude only that results provide evidence for the occurrence of amnesia for childhood traumatic experiences and subsequent recovery; their conclusions are not definitive (p. 755).

## Hulbert & Anderson (2018): full text read; experiment-level values

**Reference:** Hulbert, J. C., & Anderson, M. C. (2018). What doesn’t kill you makes you stronger: Psychological trauma and its relationship to enhanced memory control. *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, 147*(12), 1931–1949. https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0000461. [Cambridge author repository full PDF](https://api.repository.cam.ac.uk/server/api/core/bitstreams/7e0cc5d7-8991-4cad-8b05-b65ca736d1f4/content) · [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6277128/).

**Full-text access status:** Read complete 20-page author-accepted manuscript in the University of Cambridge repository (PDF identifies copyright retained by authors and Creative Commons Attribution 3.0; see opening p. 1). The repository is an author/institutional copy. Printed journal pagination was not assigned to this preprint layout; locators below use article section, figure/table and manuscript PDF page (P1 = second PDF page).

**Task and instruction:** 48 undergraduates per experiment learned arbitrary neutral cue–response word pairs; for cued Think trials they recalled/sayed the associated response, and for No-Think trials they were told to avoid thinking about or saying the response for each 4-second cue (Methods, Experiment 1, manuscript pp. 2–4; Experiment 2, pp. 7–8). They then completed same-probe (SP) and independent-probe (IP) recall tests. Sixteen suppression repetitions were compared with baseline items receiving zero repetitions. This is deliberate, cue-triggered lab inhibition of newly learned associations, not spontaneous blocking of a real autobiographical episode.

**Samples:** Each experiment had 48 analyzed undergraduates split into 24 lower-trauma and 24 higher-trauma participants. Experiment 1 classified groups using a post-task retrospective trauma survey; five additional participants were excluded (2 failed learning criterion, 2 instruction noncompliance, 1 brain damage; Methods, p. 3). Experiment 2 selected 48 from a 512-student online prescreen and used an experimenter-blind procedure; 9 extra participants were run to replace exclusions (7 learning criterion, 1 noncompliance, 1 prior TNT exposure; Methods, pp. 7–8). Thus the integrated sample was 96; trauma groups were median-split on self-reported trauma before age 18, not randomized (Integrated analyses, pp. 10–12).

**Experiment 1, exact outcomes:** For both final tests collapsed, higher-trauma participants recalled 80% of unpracticed baseline items versus 72% of No-Think items (8 percentage-point suppression-induced forgetting), F(1,24)=9.62, p=.005, ηp²=.286. Lower-trauma participants had 77% vs. 77% (0 points), not significant; trauma-group × repetition interaction F(1,24)=5.62, p=.026, ηp²=.190 (Results, manuscript p. 5; Figure 1, p. 6). Test-type nuance: for higher trauma the IP contrast trended (p=.109); lower trauma showed suppression-induced facilitation on IP (p=.037); the trauma advantage did not significantly vary by test type (p=.289; Results, pp. 6–7).

**Experiment 2, exact outcomes:** Higher-trauma participants recalled 71% baseline vs. 62% No-Think items (9-point difference), F(1,24)=10.22, p=.004, ηp²=.299. Lower-trauma participants recalled 64.5% vs. 62% (2.5 points), F(1,24)=0.87, p=.361, ηp²=.035. Here the trauma group × repetition interaction did not reach significance, F(1,24)=2.57, p=.122, ηp²=.097 (Results, manuscript pp. 8–9; Figure 2, p. 9). Group-level forgetting generalized across same and independent cues in this experiment (7-point SP, 5-point IP); authors note overall IP performance dropped from 76% in Experiment 1 to 57% in Experiment 2, p<.001 (pp. 9–10).

**Integrated analyses:** Across N=96, 16 No-Think repetitions reduced recall overall versus baseline, F(1,48)=12.51, p=.001, ηp²=.207. The trauma × repetition interaction was significant, F(1,48)=7.48, p=.009, ηp²=.135. Higher-trauma participants showed forgetting, F(1,48)=19.67, p<.001, ηp²=.291; lower-trauma participants did not. Higher-trauma participants showed significant forgetting on both SP and IP tests, and effect did not vary by test type (Results, manuscript pp. 10–11; Figures 3–4). A continuous robust correlation showed greater self-reported trauma related to forgetting on the IP measure (outlier-skipped Spearman r_s=.25, bootstrap 95% CI [.03,.45]) but not SP (r_s=.02, 95% CI [−.17,.23]); the difference between these was significant one-tailed, Meng z=1.67, p=.048 (p. 12; Fig. 4). The paper calls its data correlational (Discussion, p. 13).

**Interpretive boundary and limits:** This is direct evidence that deliberate suppression can reduce later recall of cued laboratory material and that undergraduates reporting more adversity performed better on that particular intentional-control measure. It does not establish involuntary protective blocking or event-level autobiographical amnesia. Trauma exposure was self-reported and nonrandomized; samples were small university groups, largely low in depression; the trauma measure aggregated varied event types and used a median split for the group analyses. In Experiment 2, the crucial per-experiment interaction was not statistically significant despite the same directional subgroup means; the pooled interaction and IP correlation support a modest association, not causation. Authors also explicitly note that if highly exposed participants forgot or underreported event frequency, they could be misclassified, and that a nonmonotonic relation across adversity ranges was not tested (Discussion, pp. 13–15). The study is affirmative for possible learned/available retrieval control, while its experimental instructions sharply limit analogy to automatic blocking.
