# Supportive evidence and mechanism leads — UNREVIEWED

**Status:** UNREVIEWED source-gathering notes for the parent evidence review. These are candidate papers and provisional interpretations, not a settled verdict. Scope is the narrow Video 1 question: is inability to remember a possible traumatic event *usually* due to the brain protectively blocking access? Keep reported gaps, causal mechanism, and later memory accuracy distinct. The presenter fairly allows that memories may not return and symptoms can improve anyway; does not promise accurate recovery or explicitly recommend suggestion techniques. No EMDR efficacy work was pursued.

## Candidate sources

### 1. Chu et al. (1999) — selected clinical sample reporting amnesia, recovered memories and corroboration

**Reference:** Chu, J. A., Frey, L. M., Ganzel, B. L., & Matthews, J. A. (1999). Memories of childhood abuse: Dissociation, amnesia, and corroboration. *American Journal of Psychiatry, 156*(5), 749–755. https://doi.org/10.1176/ajp.156.5.749. [PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10327909/) · [publisher full text/PDF](https://psychiatryonline.org/doi/pdf/10.1176/ajp.156.5.749).

**Type/status/access:** Peer-reviewed empirical clinical study, published 1999. Abstract and full-text PDF accessible through PubMed/publisher; full text link appears lawful publisher-hosted. No local full-text copy saved; metadata and accessible source links preserved here.

**Design/sample/measures:** 90 female inpatients in a specialist unit for trauma-related disorders. Self-report measures and structured interview covered reported childhood physical/sexual abuse, witnessing abuse, dissociative symptoms, partial/complete amnesia, recovery circumstances, suggestion and independent corroboration.

**Findings/locators:** Abstract, Results, and Conclusions (journal pp. 749–755; PDF pages 1–7): participants reporting childhood abuse had higher dissociation than those not reporting it; earlier and more frequent abuse correlated with higher dissociation; a substantial proportion reported partial or complete amnesia, with earlier onset associated with greater amnesia. Most recovered memories were first recalled outside therapy; a majority reported strong corroboration. These data are affirmative evidence that abuse reports, dissociative symptoms, and reports of amnesia can co-occur in a treatment-seeking clinical population, and that some later recalled abuse accounts may be corroborated.

**Limitations / fit:** Highly selected, all-female, treatment-seeking sample; retrospective self-report and interview; abuse and memory status are not independently established for every participant, and no control group can determine the prevalence of amnesia among all trauma survivors. Correlation does not demonstrate that protective blocking caused the gaps or that this is the usual explanation for someone unable to remember an uncertain event. A majority finding corroboration in this selected sample does not mean every recovered account is accurate.

### 2. Williams (1994) — documented abuse with later failure to recall

**Reference:** Williams, L. M. (1994). Recall of childhood trauma: A prospective study of women's memories of child sexual abuse. *Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology, 62*(6), 1167–1176. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-006X.62.6.1167. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7860814/) · [alternate full text PDF](https://www.memoiretraumatique.org/assets/files/v1/Documents-pdf/1994-Anglais-Souvenir-du-traumatisme-de-l-enfance-Linda-M-Williams.pdf).

**Type/status/access:** Peer-reviewed empirical prospective follow-up, published 1994. Full-text PDF accessible from a third-party scholarly/advocacy site; publication metadata/abstract independently surfaced through search. Avoid relying on the mirror if university library access is available. The DOI page reports article title, journal, and findings.

**Design/sample/measures:** 129 adult women with previously documented childhood sexual victimization were interviewed 17 years after the original report and asked detailed questions about abuse memory. (This is distinct from Williams’s related 1995 Journal of Traumatic Stress paper below.)

**Findings/locators:** Abstract and Results (journal pp. 1167–1176; article PDF Results): 80/129 recalled abuse at follow-up; 49/129 (38%) did not report recall of abuse previously reported. In the follow-up recall group, 16% said they had at some point forgotten; women reporting a prior forgetting period were younger at abuse and less likely to have maternal support; their accounts had the same number of discrepancies versus early-1970s records as those of continuous rememberers. The study supports the existence of later non-recall and some self-reported periods of forgetting for documented abuse; it also shows that a remembered account can be compared against prior documentation.

**Limitations / fit:** A failure to mention abuse in a later interview is not by itself proof the memory was wholly inaccessible; ordinary forgetting, reluctance/non-disclosure, wording/interview context, childhood age at event, and record limitations remain possible. The “recovered” subgroup is only those who recalled at follow-up and retrospectively described a forgotten interval. It does not test unconscious protective blocking and cannot estimate what “usually” causes an unknown individual's gap.

**Citation metadata caution:** The PubMed record and DOI resolver identify this as 1994, volume 62(6), pages 1167–1176. Some secondary pages incorrectly show 1995; use the PubMed/DOI metadata.

### 3. Williams (1995) — related paper from the same cohort, with corroboration detail

**Reference:** Williams, L. M. (1995). Recovered memories of abuse in women with documented child sexual victimization histories. *Journal of Traumatic Stress, 8*(4), 649–673. https://doi.org/10.1002/jts.2490080408. [Publisher record](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/jts.2490080408) · [alternate PDF](https://www.jimhopper.com/pdf/williams1995.pdf).

**Type/status/access:** Peer-reviewed empirical article, published 1995. Publisher abstract and accessible PDF mirror found; no local copy saved.

**Design/sample/measures:** Follow-up interview of 129 women with documented childhood sexual victimization, 17 years after the initial report.

**Findings/locators:** Abstract, journal pp. 649–673: 80 recalled abuse; among them, 16% said they had once forgotten it. The “recovered” and continuous-memory groups had the same number of discrepancies from early-1970s reports. Women in the recovered group had been younger when abused and reported less maternal support. It appears to draw on the same 129-woman follow-up cohort as Williams (1994), so treat these two articles as one evidence family rather than independent replication.

**Limitations / fit:** Same cohort/data family as source 2 (do not count as independent replication). For the argument, this is chiefly evidence about later accuracy/corroboration and a reported forgetting interval, not direct evidence about a protective mechanism. The record comparison offers some objective checks but does not establish perfect accuracy or a general rate of repression.

### 4. Chu et al. (2012) — reported recovered memory relates to dissociation, but not specifically to trauma

**Reference:** Chiu, C.-D., Yeh, Y.-Y., Ross, C. A., Lin, S.-F., Huang, W.-T., & Hwu, H.-G. (2012). Recovered memory experience in a nonclinical sample is associated with dissociation rather than with aversive experiences. *Psychiatry Research, 197*(3), 265–269. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.psychres.2011.10.002. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22370149/) · [publisher page](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0165178111006834).

**Type/status/access:** Peer-reviewed empirical cross-sectional survey, published 2012. PubMed abstract and publisher abstract/section snippets accessible; full text may require subscription. Metadata and abstracts saved here only.

**Design/sample/measures:** Three survey waves pooled; final sample 683 college students (41% male). Measures included Dissociative Experiences Scale, childhood trauma/adversity, fantasy proneness/absorption, and reported recovered-memory experiences.

**Findings/locators:** Abstract; publisher sections “Participants,” “Results,” and “Discussion” (article pp. 265–269, especially methods/results): recovered-memory experience correlated with dissociation; the association was not accounted for by measured childhood adversity, fantasy proneness, or absorption. Trauma was not significantly correlated with recovered-memory experience in this sample, and recovered events could be negative, neutral, or positive. This qualifies a simple trauma-causes-dissociative-gap account: reported delayed recall is associated with dissociation, but adversity is not necessary in these self-reports.

**Limitations / fit:** Cross-sectional, nonclinical convenience sample; all key constructs are self-reported, including the purported recovered memories, with no event verification; cannot establish causal order, mechanisms, or prevalence in survivors. Strongly relevant to the causal *specificity* question; does not refute that some trauma-related gaps occur.

### 5. Staniloiu, Markowitsch, and colleagues (2018) — clinical case series of autobiographical amnesia

**Reference:** Staniloiu, A., Markowitsch, H. J., & colleagues. (2018). Psychological causes of autobiographical amnesia: A study of 28 cases. *Neuropsychologia, 110*, 134–147. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuropsychologia.2017.10.017. [DOI/publisher abstract and sections](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuropsychologia.2017.10.017).

**Type/status/access:** Peer-reviewed empirical case-series paper, published in *Neuropsychologia* in February 2018. Abstract, Methods, Table 1 snippet, and discussion/conclusions are accessible through the publisher DOI page; full text appears subscription-limited. Author list beyond first two should be verified from DOI/library metadata before APA use. Metadata only saved locally.

**Design/sample/measures:** Clinical series of 28 patients diagnosed with psychogenic/dissociative autobiographical amnesia; comprehensive neuropsychological tests and clinical/semi-structured assessment. Ages at assessment 11–54 (mean reported 35.6). Cases were grouped by presentation and proposed trigger; Table 1 gives characteristics, triggering factor, histories and neuropsychological results.

**Findings/locators:** Publisher abstract; “Methods,” “Case reports” (Table 1), and “Discussion and conclusions” (journal pp. 134–147): all patients had severe episodic-autobiographical amnesia; the cases had mixed presentations and possible causes/consequences, including forensic context, fugue, somatic illness, childhood/youth presentation, and reduced cognitive effort. Authors conclude that psychological-origin autobiographical amnesia may occur in varied clinical pictures and state it “probably serves a protective function” by helping people exit an unmanageable/adverse situation.

**Limitations / fit:** Rare, highly selected diagnosed case series without a comparison group or population denominator; etiologic attribution and “protective function” are interpretive, not directly experimentally demonstrated. The cases support that severe autobiographical amnesia without overt brain damage is clinically described, and give a plausible adaptive interpretation in some cases. They do not establish that it is common, trauma-specific, or usually the cause of an uncertain person's missing memory. This is the clearest explicit affirmative source for the “protective” mechanism, but its inferential step must be named.

### 6. Hulbert & Anderson (2018) — trauma history associated with stronger voluntary suppression in a lab task

**Reference:** Hulbert, J. C., & Anderson, M. C. (2018). What doesn’t kill you makes you stronger: Psychological trauma and its relationship to enhanced memory control. *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, 147*(12), 1931–1949. https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0000461. [Open full text at PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6277128/) · [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30024184/).

**Type/status/access:** Peer-reviewed two-experiment empirical article, published 2018; full text is open access in PMC and author/publisher rights are stated in the article. Readable in full text; no PDF copy saved.

**Design/sample/measures:** Think/No-Think task. Undergraduates learned word-picture associations, then were instructed either to recall or directly suppress picture memories. Experiment 1: 48 undergraduates separated into higher- and lower-self-reported childhood-trauma groups; Experiment 2: another 48 undergraduates selected from a 512-student prescreen, with experimenter blinding and incentives for final recall. Main tests assessed memory for instructed “No-Think” items versus unpracticed baseline items, including same and independent cues.

**Findings/locators:** Abstract; Experiment 1/2 Methods; integrated Results; Figure 2/Table 1; Discussion (journal pp. 1931–1949): both experiments found more suppression-induced forgetting among higher-trauma-history students. This gives mechanistic plausibility that trauma experience can be associated with learned/available inhibitory control over retrieval. The authors describe suppression as consciously stopping retrieval when cued; it is an intentional lab action.

**Limitations / fit:** Trauma history was self-reported and non-randomized; undergraduates are not clinical patients; deliberately suppressing recently learned lab items is not an involuntary failure to access a whole autobiographical event, and it does not show the action is protective or happens “usually.” The work supports a narrow capacity/mechanism only. Avoid paraphrasing it as proof of repression.

### 7. Catarino et al. (2015) — countercheck: PTSD group showed impaired instructed suppression

**Reference:** Catarino, A., Küpper, C. S., Werner-Seidler, A., Dalgleish, T., & Anderson, M. C. (2015). Failing to forget: Inhibitory-control deficits compromise memory suppression in posttraumatic stress disorder. *Psychological Science, 26*(5), 604–616. https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797615569889. [Open full text at PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4426138/) · [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25847536/).

**Type/status/access:** Peer-reviewed empirical Think/No-Think experiment, 2015; open full text in PMC.

**Design/sample/measures:** 18 participants with current PTSD and 18 trauma-exposed controls without current or past PTSD (matched on trauma type/time; Table 1). Participants learned neutral object-cue/unpleasant-scene pairs and were then explicitly told to retrieve or directly suppress scenes when shown cues. The final test compared suppress items with unpracticed baseline items.

**Findings/locators:** Full-text “Participants” and Table 1; “TNT-task performance,” Fig. 3; Results and Discussion (journal pp. 604–616): suppression-induced forgetting was reliably diminished in PTSD participants versus trauma-exposed controls across identification, gist, and detail measures. Controls showed forgetting on gist/detail; PTSD participants did not, and the identification measure showed better memory for suppressed than baseline items in PTSD. Deficits correlated with symptom severity. Direction is important: this experiment does **not** support a general idea that trauma leads to effective protective forgetting; it suggests voluntary inhibitory control can be compromised in PTSD, leaving memories more accessible/intrusive.

**Limitations / fit:** Small N=36; lab pairs/scenes and explicit intentional suppression, not naturally inaccessible real-life autobiographical memories; a PTSD sample cannot establish causes of amnesia in people without PTSD. It qualifies the broad inference while being informative about mechanism: suppression is controlled and may fail, particularly in people with intrusive-memory symptoms.

### 8. Dalenberg et al. (2012) — major affirmative synthesis, not a new experiment

**Reference:** Dalenberg, C. J., Brand, B. L., Gleaves, D. H., Dorahy, M. J., Loewenstein, R. J., Cardeña, E., Frewen, P. A., Carlson, E. B., & Spiegel, D. (2012). Evaluation of the evidence for the trauma and fantasy models of dissociation. *Psychological Bulletin, 138*(3), 550–588. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0027447. [Full author-hosted PDF](https://www.towson.edu/cla/departments/psychology/facultystaff/dalenbergetalevalevidencefortraumaandfantasymodelspsychbull2012.pdf) · [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22409505/).

**Type/status/access:** Peer-reviewed theoretical review/evidence evaluation published 2012; full PDF available from first author’s university site. Not a new empirical sample. PDF/page locators below refer to printed journal page numbers.

**Method/findings/locators:** Authors evaluate eight contrasting predictions of the trauma and fantasy models and review empirical research. Abstract (p. 550): they conclude evidence strongly supports trauma as a cause of dissociation, including when fantasy proneness is controlled, and discuss dissociation as a fear/extreme-emotion regulatory response with biological correlates. Relevant sections: trauma history and dissociation (pp. 555–562); dissociation and memory fragmentation (approx. pp. 566–571); recovered-memory accuracy versus continuous memory and fantasy-model predictions (approx. pp. 575–581; verify exact page from PDF). The review identifies some prospective/corroborated cases as supporting the trauma model, while the cited studies concern broader dissociation and dissociative disorders, not prevalence of protective inability to recall an unknown event.

**Limitations / fit:** It is a scholarly review and argues a position; authors also disclose relevant clinical/theoretical orientations. Evidence pool combines varied populations, operational definitions, retrospective and prospective designs, and dissociation constructs. Strong affirmative context for trauma-linked dissociation and possible regulatory functions, but it cannot be cited as direct empirical demonstration of the speaker’s “usually” claim or of whole-event memory blockade.

### 9. Dorahy et al. (2014) — course-lead review of DID evidence and memory

**Reference:** Dorahy, M. J., Brand, B. L., Şar, V., Krüger, C., Stavropoulos, P., Martínez-Taboas, A., Lewis-Fernández, R., & Middleton, W. (2014). Dissociative identity disorder: An empirical overview. *Australian & New Zealand Journal of Psychiatry, 48*(5), 402–417. https://doi.org/10.1177/0004867414527523. [PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24788904/) · [author-accessible PDF mirror](https://umh1946.umh.es/files/2015/04/Dissociative-identity-disorder-An-empirical-overview.pdf).

**Type/status/access:** Peer-reviewed literature overview (not an experiment), published 2014. PubMed abstract and an accessible full PDF mirror located; publisher may require access.

**Method/findings/locators:** Abstract (p. 402) says review is restricted to DID-specific studies using systematic samples, psychometrically sound measures, comparisons, differential diagnosis, controls, or confirmed diagnosis. Memory-related section, especially “Memory functioning” / “Inter-identity amnesia” (PDF approx. pp. 409–411; verify precise headings/page before final citation): review discusses reported amnestic barriers and experimental work. Some dissociative patients show subjective amnesia, but controlled tasks show at least some transfer/retention of information across identity states; this cautions against treating subjective inaccessible experience as proof that traces are erased or fully segregated. A small reaction-time study of nine DID patients is mentioned in this region.

**Limitations / fit:** DID samples cannot generalize to ordinary viewers who suspect an uncertain traumatic event; overview not a systematic review by modern registration criteria; experimental memory tasks assess identity-state differences rather than trauma-caused blank periods. Useful course-supplied source for the phenomenon/mechanism, but should be described as a review and contextualized with empirical studies it cites.

## Mechanism distinctions to carry forward

- The affirmative evidence supports that (a) substantial autobiographical gaps are reported by some abuse survivors and clinical patients; (b) dissociation can co-occur with trauma history; (c) some diagnosed cases of autobiographical amnesia are interpreted by clinicians/researchers as serving an adaptive or protective role; and (d) people can sometimes intentionally inhibit access to cued material.
- These are different propositions. None of the identified studies measures the frequency with which an uncertain viewer's gap is caused by automatic protective blocking. Evidence for gaps or for deliberate inhibition does not establish the “usually” causal claim.
- The Think/No-Think papers explicitly instruct voluntary suppression of recently learned, cued items. Hulbert & Anderson (2018) find higher trauma history associated with *more* lab suppression-induced forgetting. Catarino et al. (2015) find this voluntary control *weaker* in a small PTSD sample. Both are mechanism analogies, not tests of spontaneous amnesia for an entire lived event.
- Later recall and truth are separate questions. Williams (1995) and Chu et al. (1999) offer documented/corroborated examples and similar discrepancy rates across some groups; they do not mean every later memory is accurate. They also do not prove how the prior gap arose.
- The speaker's acknowledgment that a memory may never return and symptoms may nevertheless improve is consistent with not making recollection a necessary endpoint; this is a fair point and separate from whether the protective-blocking explanation is “usually” correct.
