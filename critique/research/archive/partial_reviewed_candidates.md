# Video 1: PARTIAL Sol-reviewed source set

Status 29 September 2026: **PARTIAL**. These six published empirical papers have been checked against accessible full texts by the reviewer. Additional candidates and the full review audit are in progress. This file is safe for preliminary assessment, not the final 10–15-paper set. Here “full text read” means the article and relevant methods/results/limitations were inspected; it does not mean the result proves the video’s causal claim. The question is whether inability to remember a *possible* traumatic event is *usually* due to protective blocking.

## Williams (1994) — documented event, later nonreport

Williams, L. M. (1994). Recall of childhood trauma: A prospective study of women's memories of child sexual abuse. *Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology, 62*(6), 1167–1176. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-006X.62.6.1167. [Full article](https://www.memoiretraumatique.org/assets/files/v1/Documents-pdf/1994-Anglais-Souvenir-du-traumatisme-de-l-enfance-Linda-M-Williams.pdf); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7860814/). Published, peer-reviewed empirical cohort follow-up. **Full text read; numerical and interpretive claims independently verified** at pp. 1167, 1170–1172 (Fig. 1, Tables 1–3), 1173–1175.

129 adult women with hospital-documented childhood sexual victimization were interviewed about 17 years later. 49/129 (38%) did not report/recollect the index abuse; 80/129 did. Younger age and perpetrator familiarity were associated with nonreport. Crucial nuance: most were at least 7 at abuse; among those aged 7 or older, 28% had no recall (p. 1171, Table 1). The author tested some reluctance/record-error explanations, but could not directly observe the current accessibility of the memory. **Bearing:** strong evidence that later nonreport of documented abuse happens; no direct test of protective blocking, so it cannot establish that this is the usual cause of an uncertain person's gap. Avoid calling the 38% “repressed.”

## Williams (1995) — same cohort, retrospective forgotten interval

Williams, L. M. (1995). Recovered memories of abuse in women with documented child sexual victimization histories. *Journal of Traumatic Stress, 8*(4), 649–673. https://doi.org/10.1002/jts.2490080408. [Full article](https://www.jimhopper.com/pdf/williams1995.pdf); [publisher](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/jts.2490080408). Published, peer-reviewed empirical follow-up of **the same 129 women** as Williams (1994), so one evidence family. **Full text read; claims verified** at pp. 649, 655–656, 662–663 (Table 6), 668–670.

Of 80 who recalled index abuse, 16% (about one in ten of the total 129) retrospectively reported a past interval of forgetting. On measured discrepancies against earlier records, recovered and continuous rememberers did not differ (average 2.00 vs 1.88 inconsistent details; p. 662, Table 6). The comparison is small and retrospective; a similar discrepancy count does not validate every detail, and the original report can also contain errors. **Bearing:** some delayed memories concern documented events; memory discontinuity alone does not mean fabrication. It does not identify why the earlier gap occurred.

## Geraerts et al. (2007) — independent corroboration by recovery context

Geraerts, E., Schooler, J. W., Merckelbach, H., Jelicic, M., Hauer, B. J. A., & Ambadar, Z. (2007). The reality of recovered memories: Corroborating continuous and discontinuous memories of childhood sexual abuse. *Psychological Science, 18*(7), 564–568. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2007.01940.x. [Full article](https://sites.pitt.edu/~bertsch/Geraerts.pdf). Published, peer-reviewed empirical study. **Full text read; claims verified** at pp. 565–567, especially Method, Results, Table 1 and Discussion.

Newspaper-recruited, self-selected participants: 71 reported continuous memories; 57 reported discontinuous memories, split into spontaneous recall outside therapy (n=41) and recall in therapy (n=16). Blind raters sought reports from others meeting prespecified corroboration criteria. Corroboration: 45% continuous, 37% outside-therapy recovery, 0/16 therapy recovery (p. 565). This supports possible accuracy of some spontaneous delayed memories and a context difference. The authors explicitly state that uncorroborated does **not** imply false; corroboration itself is imperfect, and remembered discontinuity is self-reported. Recovery context was not randomized; therapy-associated differences cannot be attributed solely to therapist suggestion. **Bearing:** later accuracy varies; it cannot diagnose protective blocking or estimate its usual frequency.

## Geraerts et al. (2009) — heterogeneous cognitive profiles

Geraerts, E., Lindsay, D. S., Merckelbach, H., Jelicic, M., Raymaekers, L., Arnold, M. M., & Schooler, J. W. (2009). Cognitive mechanisms underlying recovered-memory experiences of childhood sexual abuse. *Psychological Science, 20*(1), 92–98. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02247.x. [Full article](https://onlineacademiccommunity.uvic.ca/lindsaylab/wp-content/uploads/sites/4861/2020/07/GeraertsLindsayMerkelbachJelicieRaymaekersArnoldSchooler2008.pdf); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19037903/). Published, peer-reviewed empirical comparison. **Full text read; methods and results verified** at pp. 93–96, Method, DRM/FIA task descriptions, Results/Tables 1–2, Discussion.

120 adults recruited by newspaper advertisement, 30 each in spontaneous recovery, suggestive-therapy recovery, continuous-memory, and no-abuse control groups. The suggestive-therapy group had greater *laboratory* false-word recall susceptibility; the spontaneous group more often forgot previous *laboratory* acts of remembering. Groups were classified retrospectively, and the tasks neither verify the abuse reports nor measure actual causal mechanisms of any earlier gap. The suggestive group specifically involved hypnosis/guided imagery/dream interpretation (p. 93); it is not a generic therapy comparison. **Bearing:** multiple plausible cognitive routes to a reported recovery; not a unitary protective-blocking explanation.

## Loftus & Pickrell (1995) — suggestive false autobiographical reports

Loftus, E. F., & Pickrell, J. E. (1995). The formation of false memories. *Psychiatric Annals, 25*(12), 720–725. https://doi.org/10.3928/0048-5713-19951201-07. [Author manuscript close to published version](https://www.myptsd.com/gallery/-pdf/1-96.pdf); [publisher record](https://journals.healio.com/doi/10.3928/0048-5713-19951201-07). Published, peer-reviewed empirical experiment. **Near-final full manuscript read; precise article wording/figures not independently checked against final typeset version.** Relevant manuscript sections: “Lost again,” Method, Results, Discussion (PDF pp. 2–5); publication pp. 720–725.

24 adults heard three true childhood narratives and one false “lost in a mall/public place” narrative ostensibly supplied by a family member, then completed a booklet and two interviews. Six of 24 (25%) still claimed some full **or partial** recollection after interviews; partial reports included speculation, and their clarity/detail were generally lower than true memories. The design demonstrates susceptibility under deliberate family-backed suggestion, not prevalence of false trauma memories in ordinary therapy. **Bearing:** later felt recollection is not automatic proof of event truth; this is separate from whether real events can be forgotten. Do not call the 25% vivid, complete memories.

## Catarino et al. (2015) — voluntary suppression countercheck in PTSD

Catarino, A., Küpper, C. S., Werner-Seidler, A., Dalgleish, T., & Anderson, M. C. (2015). Failing to forget: Inhibitory-control deficits compromise memory suppression in posttraumatic stress disorder. *Psychological Science, 26*(5), 604–616. https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797615569889. [Publisher full article PDF](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/pdf/10.1177/0956797615569889); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25847536/). Published, peer-reviewed empirical experiment. **Full text read; methods/result verified** at pp. 607–612 (Participants, task, Fig. 3, Discussion).

18 people with PTSD and 18 trauma-exposed controls without PTSD learned object–aversive-scene pairs, then were explicitly instructed to retrieve or suppress the associated scene. Compared with controls, the PTSD group showed diminished suppression-induced forgetting across outcome measures; worse forgetting related to greater PTSD symptoms. This is a small, nonrandomized clinical comparison using newly learned material and deliberate suppression, not a test of unconscious blocking of an actual trauma. **Bearing:** retrieval inhibition is experimentally tractable but does not always become stronger after trauma; it offers a countercheck against inferring that overwhelming events are usually kept inaccessible for protection.
