# Video 1 evidence table — Astra-selected, Sol-reviewed

**Status:** eight selected published references; six empirical papers and two scholarly reviews/commentaries. Selection followed Luna gathering → Sol source review → Astra assessment on 29 September 2026. This is a research planning aid, not an essay draft or final thesis. See [shortlist decisions](reviewed_shortlist.md), [review audit](review/review_audit.md), and [complete candidate set](review/reviewed_candidates.md).

Keep **memory gaps**, **protective retrieval blocking**, and **later-memory accuracy** separate. No selected study measures the relative causes of memory gaps in the uncertain-event population required to establish “usually.” That design limitation is not proof that protective amnesia never happens.

All eight are published in peer-reviewed scientific journals. Dalenberg is a narrative (non-systematic) review and Lynn a critical commentary/review; neither contributes a new independent experimental sample. Locators explicitly distinguish journal pages from author-manuscript PDF pages. Source-boundary notes below summarise limits visible in the methods/discussion; “Astra appraisal” is our further interpretation. Full-text source checks were performed by Sol on Luna’s extracts; Astra selected and assessed the reviewed records.

**Adversarial source review, 29 September 2026:** an adversarial existence/credibility review was done on all eight sources. Existence and bibliographic details were confirmed for each; page locators, table/figure numbers and some quoted figures were not all re-checked and are marked “unverified” below (check against the PDF before quoting). <a id="williams-1994"></a>

## Williams (1994)

**Full reference, status, access and verification:** Williams, L. M. (1994). Recall of childhood trauma: A prospective study of women's memories of child sexual abuse. *Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology, 62*(6), 1167–1176. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-006X.62.6.1167. [Full article](https://www.memoiretraumatique.org/assets/files/v1/Documents-pdf/1994-Anglais-Souvenir-du-traumatisme-de-l-enfance-Linda-M-Williams.pdf); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7860814/). Published, peer-reviewed empirical cohort follow-up. **Credibility 4/5.** Existence confirmed (JCCP 62(6), 1167–1176, DOI 10.1037/0022-006X.62.6.1167); issue/erratum not checked on PsycNET. Full text was read earlier, but the numerical and locator claims below (pp. 1170–1175, Tables 1–3, Fig. 1) are **unverified — check against the PDF**. Prefer the publisher DOI as primary access; the memoiretraumatique.org PDF is a third-party copy.

**Design, sample and measures:** Prospective follow-up of 129 adult women approximately 17 years after hospital documentation of childhood sexual victimization. Interview report of the documented index event was compared with earlier records.

**Reported findings and locators:** 49/129 (38%) did not report the index abuse; 80 did. Among those aged at least 7 at abuse, 28% did not recall it (age-7+/28% figure unverified — check against PDF). Younger age and perpetrator familiarity were associated with nonreport (pp. 1170–1172, especially p. 1171/Table 1; Tables 2–3; locators unverified).

**Source limitations/boundaries:** The author examines reluctance to disclose and record errors as alternatives; this does not directly establish each participant’s current memory accessibility (pp. 1173–1175; locator unverified). Critique: Loftus, E. F., Garry, M., & Feldman, J. (1994). Forgetting sexual trauma: What does it mean when 38% forget? *Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology, 62*(6), 1177–1181. They argue nonreport may reflect nondisclosure or infantile amnesia rather than inaccessible memory.

**Astra appraisal:** Nonreport is an observable interview outcome, whereas an intact but blocked memory is an inferred state. The selected documented-abuse cohort does not estimate causes of gaps among people unsure whether an event occurred. Infantile amnesia cannot simply explain away the older-child subgroup.

**Precise bearing on claim:** Supports the narrow proposition that documented events can go unreported much later. Leaves protective blocking and its usual frequency unresolved.

**Tentative essay role:** Affirmative evidence: establish the phenomenon, then distinguish nonreport from mechanism.

## Hulbert & Anderson (2018)

**Full reference, status, access and verification:** Hulbert, J. C., & Anderson, M. C. (2018). What doesn’t kill you makes you stronger: Psychological trauma and its relationship to enhanced memory control. *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, 147*(12), 1931–1949. https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0000461. [Cambridge repository full article](https://api.repository.cam.ac.uk/server/api/core/bitstreams/7e0cc5d7-8991-4cad-8b05-b65ca736d1f4/content); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30024184/). Published empirical two-experiment study. **Credibility 4/5.** Publication details verified (JEP: General 147(12), 1931–1949). Full author article read by Luna; Sol checked task description, integrated test, and experiment-2 caveat in full text (pp. 2–3, 9–13, Figures 1–4 are advance-online author-PDF pages, not journal pages). Anderson, a co-author, developed the think/no-think paradigm.

**Design, sample and measures:** Two Think/No-Think experiments, each with 48 undergraduates (24 higher and 24 lower self-reported childhood-trauma history; N=96 across experiments). Participants deliberately retrieved or suppressed recently learned cue–response word pairs. Same-cue and independent-cue tests assessed later recall.

**Reported findings and locators:** The reviewed integrated analysis showed a trauma-history × suppression-repetition interaction, p=.009, favouring greater suppression-induced forgetting in the higher-trauma group; the corresponding interaction in Experiment 2 alone was not significant, p=.122 (advance-online author PDF pp. 9–13, Figures 1–4). Sol’s final check confirmed this analysis pooled N=96 across both experiments and collapsed negative and neutral targets. The paper reports F(1,48)=7.48; 48 is its reported error degree of freedom, not the participant count. Its exact degree-of-freedom construction was not explicit, so no further explanation is inferred (author PDF pp. 10–12).

**Source limitations/boundaries:** Trauma history was self-reported and groups were not randomly assigned. The measure is a self-reported count of traumatic event types before age 18 (Goldberg & Freyd), not PTSD or clinical status. The experiments concern deliberate suppression of newly learned words; Experiment 2’s standalone result qualifies the integrated result (author PDF pp. 9–13). The Experiment 2 interaction (p=.122) was accompanied by no significant Repetition × Trauma × Experiment interaction, and the authors treat the effect as consistent across experiments. The lower-trauma group showed no reliable suppression-induced forgetting even when pooled, and the higher-trauma advantage did not interact with valence.

**Astra appraisal:** This is a mechanistic analogy, not observation of an involuntarily inaccessible autobiographical trauma. Group differences cannot establish trauma caused improved memory control or that the control served a protective function.

**Precise bearing on claim:** Supports the possibility of retrieval control and a limited trauma-history association. Does not establish the automatic, whole-event, usual-cause explanation.

**Tentative essay role:** Affirmative mechanism evidence, paired with Catarino; avoid turning a laboratory effect into a diagnosis.

## Catarino et al. (2015)

**Full reference, status, access and verification:** Catarino, A., Küpper, C. S., Werner-Seidler, A., Dalgleish, T., & Anderson, M. C. (2015). Failing to forget: Inhibitory-control deficits compromise memory suppression in posttraumatic stress disorder. *Psychological Science, 26*(5), 604–616. https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797615569889. [Publisher full article PDF](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/pdf/10.1177/0956797615569889); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25847536/). Published, peer-reviewed empirical experiment. **Credibility 4/5.** Publication details verified (Psychological Science 26(5), 604–616). Full text read; the pp. 607–612 and Fig. 3 locators are **unverified — check against the PDF**.

**Design, sample and measures:** 18 people with PTSD and 18 trauma-exposed controls without PTSD learned object–aversive-scene pairs and completed a think/no-think task (recall vs suppress cue). Clinical status was observational; the material and retrieval instructions were experimental.

**Reported findings and locators:** The PTSD group showed diminished suppression-induced forgetting relative to trauma-exposed controls, with the largest deficits among the most symptomatic patients; the within-group correlation with symptoms was only marginal (pp. 607–612, Participants, task, Fig. 3 and Discussion; locators unverified).

**Source limitations/boundaries:** The clinical comparison is small and uses newly learned material under deliberate instructions rather than actual autobiographical trauma; the scenes are generic IAPS aversive images, not personal memories. Causal direction is unknown: the deficit may predate PTSD (pp. 607–612; locator unverified).

**Astra appraisal:** This does not directly contradict Hulbert and Anderson: the populations and clinical status differ. It shows why trauma exposure and PTSD cannot be treated as one uniform memory-control condition.

**Precise bearing on claim:** Qualifies a general protective-blocking account: suppression is not uniformly enhanced after trauma. It does not show protective amnesia is impossible.

**Tentative essay role:** Counterevidence beside Hulbert and Anderson; explain the population/task differences briefly.

## Geraerts et al. (2007)

**Full reference, status, access and verification:** Geraerts, E., Schooler, J. W., Merckelbach, H., Jelicic, M., Hauer, B. J. A., & Ambadar, Z. (2007). The reality of recovered memories: Corroborating continuous and discontinuous memories of childhood sexual abuse. *Psychological Science, 18*(7), 564–568. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2007.01940.x. [Full article](https://sites.pitt.edu/~bertsch/Geraerts.pdf). Published, peer-reviewed empirical study. **Credibility 4/5.** Publication details verified (Psychological Science 18(7), 564–567). Full text read; claims verified at pp. 565–567, especially Method, Results, Table 1 and Discussion.

**Integrity note (both Geraerts papers):** the retracted paper is Geraerts et al. (2008), *Memory* (“Linking thought suppression and recovered memories of childhood sexual abuse”), retracted in 2013 for data inconsistencies ([Retraction Watch](https://retractionwatch.com/2013/07/24/bad-memory-repressed-sexual-abuse-memory-paper-retracted-for-data-inconsistencies/)). Neither the 2007 nor the 2009 paper is implicated, and no retraction or correction was found for either. No misconduct finding is made or implied against Geraerts.

**Design, sample and measures:** Self-selected newspaper sample: 71 continuous rememberers and 57 discontinuous rememberers, including 41 spontaneous outside-therapy recoveries and 16 in-therapy recoveries. Blind raters assessed third-party evidence using specified corroboration criteria.

**Reported findings and locators:** Corroboration rates were similar for continuous memories (45%) and spontaneous outside-therapy recovery (37%; the authors say “relatively” similar), and 0/16 for in-therapy recovery (pp. 565–567, Method, Results/Table 1).

**Source limitations/boundaries:** The authors explicitly state that lack of corroboration does not mean falsity. Corroboration is imperfect, and memory discontinuity is retrospectively self-reported. The authors also note that newspaper-recruited adults may not generalise to clinical samples (Discussion, pp. 566–567).

**Astra appraisal:** Context was not randomized; these differences do not isolate a causal effect of therapy or suggestion. Corroborated event occurrence does not validate every detail or identify why an earlier gap occurred.

**Precise bearing on claim:** Supports possible accuracy of some delayed memories while qualifying blanket confidence in recovery. Tests corroboration, not protective blocking.

**Tentative essay role:** Accuracy qualification: neither automatically accept nor dismiss a recovered memory; keep therapy claims observational.

## Geraerts et al. (2009)

**Full reference, status, access and verification:** Geraerts, E., Lindsay, D. S., Merckelbach, H., Jelicic, M., Raymaekers, L., Arnold, M. M., & Schooler, J. W. (2009). Cognitive mechanisms underlying recovered-memory experiences of childhood sexual abuse. *Psychological Science, 20*(1), 92–98. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02247.x. [Full article](https://onlineacademiccommunity.uvic.ca/lindsaylab/wp-content/uploads/sites/4861/2020/07/GeraertsLindsayMerkelbachJelicieRaymaekersArnoldSchooler2008.pdf); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19037903/). Published, peer-reviewed empirical comparison. **Credibility 4/5.** Publication details verified (Psychological Science 20(1), 92–98). Full text read, but the checked copy was a proof with its own pagination, so the pp. 93–96 and Tables 1–2 locators are **unverified — check against the published PDF**. See the integrity note under Geraerts et al. (2007): the retracted paper is the 2008 *Memory* paper, not this one.

**Design, sample and measures:** 120 advertisement-recruited adults, 30 each in spontaneous recovery, suggestive-therapy recovery, continuous-memory and no-abuse control groups. Laboratory DRM false recall/recognition and forgotten-prior-remembering tasks were compared (pp. 93–96, Tables 1–2; locators unverified).

**Reported findings and locators:** The suggestive-therapy group showed greater laboratory DRM false recall/recognition susceptibility; spontaneous recoverers more often forgot earlier laboratory acts of remembering. The suggestive methods included hypnosis, guided imagery and dream interpretation (p. 93; results pp. 94–96).

**Source limitations/boundaries:** Groups are retrospective and laboratory outcomes do not independently verify participants’ reported abuse or earlier memory histories (Method/Discussion, pp. 93–96; locators unverified). That “lab outcomes do not verify abuse” is this essay’s appraisal, not a stated authors’ limitation.

**Astra appraisal:** The results identify possible cognitive correlates, not established causal explanations of individual recoveries. False word-list recall is not autobiographical false-memory implantation. The specific suggestive group cannot represent all therapy, EMDR, or this speaker’s practice.

**Precise bearing on claim:** Qualifies a single-mechanism account of reported recovery; supports considering more than one explanation without declaring actual memories false.

**Tentative essay role:** Alternative explanations: apparent recovery can have heterogeneous cognitive correlates.

## Dalenberg et al. (2012)

**Full reference, status, access and verification:** Dalenberg, C. J., Brand, B. L., Gleaves, D. H., Dorahy, M. J., Loewenstein, R. J., Cardeña, E., Frewen, P. A., Carlson, E. B., & Spiegel, D. (2012). Evaluation of the evidence for the trauma and fantasy models of dissociation. *Psychological Bulletin, 138*(3), 550–588. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0027447. [University-hosted full article](https://www.towson.edu/cla/departments/psychology/facultystaff/dalenbergetalevalevidencefortraumaandfantasymodelspsychbull2012.pdf). Published narrative (non-systematic) review, **not a meta-analysis and not a new experiment**. **Credibility 3.5/5.** Publication details verified (Psychological Bulletin 138(3), 550–588). Authors are trauma-model proponents (author allegiance). Full relevant text was checked by Sol, but the pp. 550, 565–567 locators are **unverified — check against the PDF**.

**Design, sample and measures:** Published review/evidence synthesis comparing eight predictions of trauma and fantasy models of dissociation. No single new sample or experimental manipulation; it incorporates prior studies.

**Reported findings and locators:** The authors report a consistent, moderate trauma–dissociation association, also with objective trauma measures, interpreting dissociation as a possible response to extreme emotion. Their recovered-memory discussion includes corroborated cases and the Geraerts therapy-group null result (pp. 550, 565–567 and conclusions).

**Source limitations/boundaries:** The review covers broad dissociation and draws on heterogeneous studies; its discussion includes limits to corroboration. The causal interpretation is explicitly disputed in the later commentary.

**Astra appraisal:** Broad dissociation is not equivalent to a completely inaccessible event memory. Williams and Geraerts findings cited here are the same evidence already counted, not replications. No new general-population cause-frequency estimate is supplied.

**Precise bearing on claim:** Provides the strongest course-linked affirmative interpretation while falling short of directly verifying the speaker’s bounded claim.

**Tentative essay role:** Brief affirmative framework; use empirical sources for numerical findings. Read alongside Lynn rather than treating this as settled consensus.

## Lynn et al. (2014)

**Full reference, status, access and verification:** Lynn, S. J., Lilienfeld, S. O., Merckelbach, H., Giesbrecht, T., McNally, R. J., Loftus, E. F., Bruck, M., Garry, M., & Malaktaris, A. (2014). The trauma model of dissociation: Inconvenient truths and stubborn fictions. Comment on Dalenberg et al. (2012). *Psychological Bulletin, 140*(3), 896–910. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0035570. [Harvard open author article](https://dash.harvard.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/7312037d-d28e-6bd4-e053-0100007fdf3b/content); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24773505/). Published critical commentary/review, not an independent trauma-memory experiment. **Credibility 3.5/5.** Publication details verified (Psychological Bulletin 140(3), 896–910, DOI 10.1037/a0035570). Authors are sceptics of the trauma model (author allegiance). The author PDF pp. 1–3, 23–27 (Prediction 7), 48–49 (corroboration caveat) locators are **unverified — check against the PDF**.

**Design, sample and measures:** Published critical commentary/review of existing correlational evidence responding to Dalenberg et al. (2012); not an independent cohort or trauma-memory experiment.

**Reported findings and locators:** Challenges causal inferences from trauma–dissociation associations and interpretations of inaccessible trauma memories, while allowing trauma may cause some dissociation (**unverified against full text**: the abstract reads as a stronger rejection, and the “concession” is asserted in Dalenberg et al.’s reply) and uncorroborated memories need not be false (author PDF pp. 1–3, 23–27; pp. 48–49/endnote 6; locators unverified).

**Source limitations/boundaries:** The commentary relies on existing correlational data and cannot resolve causal direction. Reply: Dalenberg, C. J., Brand, B. L., Loewenstein, R. J., Gleaves, D. H., Dorahy, M. J., Cardeña, E., Frewen, P. A., Carlson, E. B., & Spiegel, D. (2014). Reality versus fantasy: Reply to Lynn et al. (2014). *Psychological Bulletin, 140*(3), 911–920. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0036685 (abstract-only check). Its main rebuttal: the omitted studies failed the inclusion criteria; Lynn et al. applied inconsistent standards; trauma acts via multiple pathways; suggestibility–dissociation links are weak; and Lynn et al. conceded trauma is a causal risk factor. Cite the reply alongside Lynn et al.

**Astra appraisal:** This is a critical interpretation, not experimental proof against protective amnesia. Present the dispute fairly and do not imply the commentary had no response. Do not multiply evidence counts by treating competing reviews of the same studies as new data.

**Precise bearing on claim:** Qualifies the causal inference from trauma/dissociation to protective blocking; supplies no prevalence estimate either.

**Tentative essay role:** Critical synthesis paired with Dalenberg; foreground primary evidence and avoid retelling the entire dissociation debate.

## Anderson et al. (2025)

**Full reference, status, access and verification:** Anderson, K.-A. S., Miller, Q. C., Strange, D., & London, K. (2025). Repressed memories and the body keeps the score: Public perceptions and prevalence. *Memory, 33*(5), 495–509. https://doi.org/10.1080/09658211.2025.2479503. [Course full article](../../course/week-06/6-2.pdf); [publisher](https://doi.org/10.1080/09658211.2025.2479503). Published empirical survey. **Credibility 3.5/5 (about 4 for audience beliefs, 2 as mechanism evidence).** Publication details verified (Memory 33(5), 495–509, DOI 10.1080/09658211.2025.2479503); the published version, not a preprint. Complete local article available; Sol checked methods, Table 2, results, and discussion, but the journal pp. 497–504 and Table 2 locators are **unverified — check against the PDF** (course PDF pp. 4–10).

**Design, sample and measures:** Online survey of 1,581 US adults, described by the authors as a “nationally representative online survey” (the “Prolific” platform is unconfirmed — check against course/week-06/6-2.pdf; online panels are non-probability samples, so representativeness is the authors’ description), randomized to direct abuse wording or descriptions of unwanted experiences. Follow-ups separated continuous remembering, reinterpretation, prior knowledge without memory, and neither prior knowledge nor memory.

**Reported findings and locators:** 57/1,581 (3.6%) reported recovery with neither prior knowledge nor prior memory. 93.9% endorsed the possibility of repression for years followed by recovery. Wording affected endorsement (journal pp. 497–504, especially Table 2 and pp. 499, 504; supplied PDF pp. 4–11; locators unverified). Abstract figures: about 75% mean confidence in recovered memories; 77% endorse “the body keeps the score.”

**Source limitations/boundaries:** These are public beliefs and retrospective reports, not verified abuse histories or demonstrated repression. The wording manipulation affects reported responses, not an observed traumatic-memory mechanism (Methods/Discussion).

**Astra appraisal:** This sample was not exposed to the selected video. Neither endorsement nor reported consequences prove harm caused by its message, and a US online sample is not an unrestricted population estimate, whatever its described representativeness.

**Precise bearing on claim:** Provides empirical audience context. The 3.6% is not a protective-amnesia prevalence estimate; the 93.9% is a belief measure, not scientific support.

**Tentative essay role:** Audience implications: explain why calibrated causal language matters, while treating actual video effects as untested.
