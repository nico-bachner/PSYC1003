# 1. Plan the essay

## Claim and reviewed thesis

Video 1 says inability to remember **whether** something traumatic happened usually reflects protective blocking. It also allows that memories may not return and symptoms may improve regardless. Neither guaranteed accuracy nor specific suggestive techniques are stated.

Thesis revised following user review:

> Although documented nonreport, corroborated delayed recollections and laboratory suppression findings lend plausibility to the video's explanation, the reviewed studies do not establish that protective blocking usually causes uncertainty about a traumatic event. Their methodological limits and alternative explanations make the video's causal reassurance scientifically overstated, despite its potentially helpful message that improvement need not depend on recovering memories.

The central distinction is between evidence that an event occurred, evidence of a later gap, the cause of that gap, and accuracy of subsequent recollection. Keep the verdict bounded to the [six-source evidence record](../02-research/evidence_table.md).

Give the affirmative evidence its strongest fair interpretation before explaining its limits. Distinguish counterevidence from insufficient evidence: the studies do not establish the usual-cause claim, but neither show that protective blocking is impossible nor establish another explanation as usual.

These allocations are planning targets, not assessment rules; total 920 words including citations and subheadings.

| Audience implications | 150 | Credit reassurance and improvement without recovery; explain possible unwarranted certainty about an unknown event. Use Anderson for belief context only. State the on-balance judgement: the reassurance has value, but presenting an uncertain explanation as the usual cause makes the message more misleading than helpful overall. Frame this as an assessment of communication, not a measured net psychological harm or video effect. |
| Conclusion | 80 | Bring together the qualified scientific verdict and audience judgement; distinguish empathy from confirming a hidden event, with no new evidence. |
