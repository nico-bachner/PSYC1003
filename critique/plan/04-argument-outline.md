## Proposed working thesis

**29 September 2026 — proposed for user review, not user-approved.**

> Although documented trauma can go unreported and some delayed memories are corroborated, the reviewed evidence does not establish that protective blocking usually explains uncertainty about a possible traumatic event. By distinguishing nonreport from mechanism, weighing retrieval-suppression findings and alternative explanations, and separating later-memory accuracy from cause, this essay argues that the video's reassuring message overstates what research can establish.

The [thesis synthesis](../research/thesis_synthesis.md) compares alternatives and records the counterargument, response, source locators and limitations. The verdict concerns the speaker's **usual-cause explanation**; it does not deny all protective blocking or label later memories generally false. Refer to the [evidence table](../research/evidence_table.md) for verified methods/results and full references.

## Six-paragraph outline

### 1. Introduction — 120 words

Establish why explanations of uncertain trauma memories matter to people's understanding of their histories. Identify **Video 1** and paraphrase its claim precisely: inability to remember whether something traumatic happened is usually protective blocking. Introduce the proposed thesis and argumentative route. Use the transcript, not a scientific paper, to establish what the speaker says. Do not imply that a reviewed paper was the speaker's source.

### 2. Supporting evidence: gaps and later accuracy — 210 words

**Purpose:** Establish the strongest real-world support before identifying what remains unproven. Briefly introduce Dalenberg et al. (2012) as the affirmative trauma/dissociation interpretation, then let primary findings carry the paragraph.

Use Williams (1994): 129 women followed about 17 years after documented childhood sexual victimisation; 38% did not report the index event. Explain that documentation strengthens evidence for event occurrence, but nonreport does not distinguish blocked access from other reasons for nonreport. Include the 28% figure for those aged 7+ if invoking childhood amnesia; do not dismiss the finding as entirely infantile amnesia.

Add Geraerts et al. (2007): independent raters found corroboration for 37% of spontaneous recoveries and 45% of continuous memories in a self-selected sample. This supports some accurate delayed recollection, but retrospective recovery histories do not establish earlier protective blocking. If mentioning the 0/16 in-therapy cell, immediately explain that uncorroborated does not mean false and context was not randomised. Prefer omitting that cell if it distracts from the causal question.

### 3. Contradictory/qualifying evidence: mechanism and alternatives — 230 words

**Purpose:** Test the move from plausible retrieval control to a general explanation. Begin with the strongest affirmative mechanism evidence so the comparison remains fair.

Hulbert and Anderson (2018) used deliberate suppression of new word pairs in two experiments (N=96); their pooled analysis favoured greater suppression-induced forgetting in the higher self-reported trauma group. Note the nonsignificant corresponding interaction in Experiment 2; exact F statistics are unnecessary. Catarino et al. (2015) found less suppression-induced forgetting in 18 PTSD participants than 18 trauma-exposed controls. Different populations qualify generalisation rather than constitute a direct failed replication. Explicit instructions and newly learned material do not establish automatic blocking of autobiographical trauma, a protective benefit, or causal effects of trauma history.

Briefly use Geraerts et al. (2009), four retrospectively classified groups of 30: spontaneous recoverers more often forgot prior laboratory remembering. This offers an alternative to sustained inaccessibility but does not establish the cause of their real memory histories. If mentioning elevated false-word recall in the suggestive-therapy group, distinguish that laboratory measure from actual abuse-memory falsity and from this speaker's practice. Do not rank alternative causes by frequency.

### 4. Critical synthesis: why “usually” is the decisive overreach — 130 words

**Purpose:** Answer the strongest counterargument rather than repeat results. Concede that documented events, corroboration and laboratory control jointly make the protective account worth considering. Then explain that evidence for each separate component does not establish their causal conjunction or its frequency in people unsure an event occurred.

Briefly pair Dalenberg et al. (2012) with Lynn et al. (2014) on the disputed causal interpretation; neither is an independent empirical sample, and broad dissociation is not equivalent to whole-event amnesia. Acknowledge the subsequent reply if describing the exchange; its unaudited contents cannot be adjudicated here. Ground the verdict in primary-study design limits. No selected study supplies the relevant denominator and comparison of causes. Conclude “not established,” not “impossible.”

### 5. Critical analysis: audience benefits and harms — 130 words

**Purpose:** Give an explicit, proportionate judgement of the message. Acknowledge the speaker says memories may not return and symptoms may improve regardless. That framing may reassure viewers and reduce pressure to recover memories; no efficacy claim needs testing here.

Use Anderson et al. (2025) briefly: 93.9% of 1,581 US online respondents endorsed possible long-term repression/recovery. This contextualises a receptive audience, not the truth of the belief or an effect of the video. Argue that presenting blocking as usual could encourage unwarranted certainty that an event occurred. On balance, the explanation poses a greater risk of misleading inference than its reassurance resolves **as scientific communication**; measured net harm is unknown. Use “may/could,” not claims that the video induces false memories or causes relationship harm. Omit the 3.6% recovery-report figure unless needed; it is not mechanism prevalence.

### 6. Conclusion — 100 words

Restate the distinction between documented nonreport, a demonstrated protective mechanism, the frequency implied by “usually,” and later-memory accuracy. Summarise that narrower evidence warrants empathy and openness to accurate delayed recollections, while the general causal explanation exceeds the reviewed findings. Reaffirm the value of reassurance without treating uncertainty as confirmation of a hidden event. Introduce no new study, treatment claim or recommendation.
