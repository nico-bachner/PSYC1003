Explanations of uncertain memories can influence how people understand their histories and interpret distress. In Video 1, a speaker identifying as an EMDR therapist explains that inability to remember whether something traumatic happened usually reflects the brain protectively blocking access to an overwhelming memory. This explanation assumes both an event and a particular cause of its inaccessibility. Although documented trauma can go unreported and some delayed recollections are corroborated, the reviewed evidence does not establish protective blocking as the usual explanation for uncertainty about a possible event. Distinguishing nonreport from mechanism, comparing laboratory suppression with alternative explanations, and separating recovered-memory accuracy from earlier forgetting reveals why the video's reassuring message exceeds its evidential support.

## Evidence supporting the explanation

Williams (1994) interviewed 129 women approximately 17 years after documented childhood sexual victimisation; 38% did not report the index event. Earlier documentation strengthens evidence that an event occurred independently of adult recollection. Nonreport also occurred among women abused at age seven or older, so infantile amnesia cannot explain the entire finding. Williams also examined reluctance to disclose: many women who did not report the index event disclosed other abuse. This weakens an explanation based on general unwillingness to discuss abuse, although selective nondisclosure remains possible. The interview outcome cannot conclusively distinguish inaccessible memory from nondisclosure or ordinary forgetting. Evidence about later accuracy is similarly qualified. In a self-selected sample, Geraerts et al. (2007) used independent raters to assess corroboration, finding it for 37% of spontaneous recoveries outside therapy and 45% of continuously remembered abuse. Some delayed recollections therefore have independent support; they should not be dismissed merely because they emerged later. Nevertheless, retrospective accounts of earlier forgetting do not demonstrate protective blocking, and corroboration does not verify every remembered detail. Conversely, absence of corroboration does not establish falsity. Together, these studies support taking reported gaps seriously while leaving their causes unresolved.

## Mechanisms and alternative explanations

Experimental retrieval suppression offers a more direct mechanism analogy. Across two experiments involving 96 undergraduates, Hulbert and Anderson (2018) instructed participants to suppress newly learned word pairs. Their pooled analysis showed greater suppression-induced forgetting among those reporting more childhood trauma, although the corresponding interaction in Experiment 2 alone was nonsignificant. This supports deliberate memory control, but nonrandom trauma-history groups cannot establish that trauma caused stronger control. Moreover, Catarino et al. (2015) found less suppression-induced forgetting in 18 participants with posttraumatic stress disorder than in 18 trauma-exposed controls using newly learned object–aversive-scene pairs. Different populations make this a qualification, rather than a direct failed replication. Both studies concern explicit instructions and recent laboratory material, leaving automatic blocking of autobiographical trauma and its protective benefit untested. An alternative concerns remembering previous remembering. Geraerts et al. (2009) compared four retrospectively classified groups of 30 adults using laboratory memory tasks. Participants reporting spontaneous recovery more often forgot earlier acts of remembering in the laboratory. This suggests that an apparent period of total forgetting might sometimes reflect forgetting previous retrievals. However, these cognitive differences neither establish what caused participants' real memory histories nor show their abuse memories were false. None of these studies determines which explanation is most frequent among people uncertain whether an event occurred.

## Weighing the claim and its implications

The strongest defence of the video is convergence: documented nonreport, corroborated recovery and experimental retrieval control each support part of a possible blocking account. Yet separate findings do not demonstrate the complete causal sequence or its frequency. Broad dissociation also includes experiences beyond whole-event amnesia. The decisive limitation remains the designs themselves: no selected study compares the relative causes of memory gaps in the population addressed by the video's usual-cause claim. The evidence therefore warrants uncertainty about that generalisation, rather than a conclusion that protective blocking is impossible or that another cause usually explains such gaps.

The message nevertheless offers reassurance. The speaker explicitly acknowledges that memories may not return and that symptoms may improve regardless. This could reduce pressure to recover memories; the video neither guarantees their accuracy nor explicitly prescribes suggestive recovery techniques. However, presenting blocking as usual could encourage viewers to interpret uncertainty as confirmation of hidden trauma. Anderson et al. (2025) found that 93.9% of respondents answering a belief item in a US online survey endorsed long-term repression followed by recovery, suggesting a receptive audience for this explanation. That result measures belief, not the mechanism's prevalence, and the study did not test this video. On balance, its unwarranted causal certainty makes the message misleading as scientific communication despite its reassuring elements. This is an assessment of the explanation's evidential quality, not a measured finding of net psychological harm.

## Conclusion

The reviewed research supports documented-event nonreport, some corroborated delayed recollections and deliberate retrieval control. It does not establish that protective blocking usually explains uncertainty about a possible traumatic event. Laboratory findings require cautious generalisation, and later-memory accuracy cannot identify an earlier forgetting mechanism. The video's acknowledgement that recovery is unnecessary for possible symptom improvement is valuable, but does not resolve its central causal overstatement. Reassurance can recognise distress and uncertainty without treating a memory gap as evidence that an unknown event occurred or that the brain blocked it for protection.
