## Introduction

Uncertainty about a possible traumatic experience can make explanations of memory especially consequential: they may shape what people believe happened to them. In Video 1, a speaker identifying as an EMDR therapist argues that this uncertainty usually occurs because the brain blocks an overwhelming memory for protection. Research on trauma and forgetting gives this account some plausibility, but does not establish its central generalisation. This essay examines evidence of documented events going unreported and deliberate memory suppression, then considers conflicting findings and alternative explanations. It argues that the video's causal claim is scientifically overstated and potentially misleading, despite its reassuring message that improvement need not depend on remembering.

## Body

### Affirmative

Evidence that genuine traumatic events are not always reported later provides support for part of the speaker's account. Williams (1994) interviewed 129 women approximately 17 years after documented childhood sexual victimisation; 38% did not report the previously recorded event. The earlier records supplied evidence independent of adult recollection. Among women aged seven or older when the event occurred, 28% did not report it, so infantile amnesia cannot explain the entire finding. These results challenge the assumption that a traumatic event will necessarily be reported many years later.

Research on delayed recollections also supports taking apparent memory gaps seriously. Geraerts et al. (2007) recruited adults reporting continuous or recovered memories of childhood sexual abuse and used independent raters to assess corroboration. Supporting evidence was found for 37% of spontaneous recoveries outside therapy, compared with 45% of continuous memories. Some delayed recollections therefore have independent support and cannot reasonably be dismissed simply because they emerged later.

A possible mechanism comes from Hulbert and Anderson (2018). Across two experiments involving 96 undergraduates, participants retrieved or suppressed newly learned word pairs. The combined analysis showed greater suppression-induced forgetting among those reporting more childhood trauma, although the corresponding interaction in the second experiment alone was not statistically significant. These findings suggest that deliberately keeping information out of awareness can reduce later accessibility. Together with documented nonreport and corroborated recovery, this gives the protective-blocking explanation a plausible scientific basis.

### Negative

The link between trauma and effective suppression is challenged by Catarino et al. (2015). Eighteen participants with posttraumatic stress disorder showed less suppression-induced forgetting than 18 trauma-exposed controls without the disorder, using learned object–distressing-scene associations. This cautions against assuming a uniform protective response to trauma. It is not a direct contradiction of Hulbert and Anderson (2018), whose groups reflected trauma history rather than clinical diagnosis. The small clinical comparison also leaves causal direction uncertain.

Alternative cognitive processes further challenge a single explanation for apparent recovery. Geraerts et al. (2009) compared four groups of 30 adults: spontaneous recoverers, people reporting recovery during suggestive therapy, continuous rememberers, and controls reporting no abuse. On laboratory tasks, spontaneous recoverers more often forgot previous acts of remembering, while the suggestive-therapy group was more susceptible to false word-list memories. An apparent period of forgetting might therefore sometimes involve forgetting earlier recollections. However, these group differences establish neither the accuracy nor the causes of participants' autobiographical memories. Findings about suggestive therapy also cannot be attributed to the video speaker, who specifies no such techniques.

The affirmative studies have additional limits. Williams's (1994) interview outcome cannot distinguish inaccessible memory from selective nondisclosure or ordinary forgetting. In Geraerts et al. (2007), corroboration supports event occurrence but does not explain earlier apparent forgetting; recovery histories were retrospective and participants were self-selected. Similarly, Hulbert and Anderson's (2018) nonrandom trauma-history groups cannot establish that trauma strengthened memory control. Deliberate suppression of recent laboratory material does not demonstrate automatic blocking of autobiographical trauma or a protective benefit.

### Critical Analysis

The strongest defence of the video is that several kinds of evidence converge: events can go unreported, delayed recollections can have corroboration, and suppression can impair recall. However, these findings support different parts of a possible account rather than establish its complete causal sequence. Evidence that an event occurred is distinct from evidence explaining why it was not reported. Most decisively, none of the studies compares the relative causes of uncertainty in the population addressed by the video. Consequently, they do not justify presenting protective blocking as the usual explanation. This conclusion concerns insufficient support for the generalisation, rather than proof that blocking never occurs.

For viewers without scientific training, the explanation could turn uncertainty into confidence that hidden trauma occurred. Anderson et al. (2025) found that 93.9% of respondents answering a US online survey item endorsed long-term repression followed by recovery. This suggests a receptive belief context, not that repression is common or that the video changes beliefs. The speaker nevertheless acknowledges that memories may never return and symptoms may improve regardless, potentially reducing pressure to remember. On balance, the message risks more harm than benefit by encouraging an unsupported interpretation of viewers' histories, despite this reassurance. That is a reasoned assessment of its communication risks, not a measured finding about the video's psychological effects. A more defensible message would acknowledge distress while preserving uncertainty about both the event and the cause of the memory gap.

## Conclusion

The evidence supports documented-event nonreport, some corroborated delayed recollections, and deliberate memory suppression. It does not establish protective blocking as the usual cause of uncertainty about a possible traumatic experience. Conflicting suppression findings, alternative cognitive processes and methodological limits prevent that inference. Although the video's reassurance that improvement need not depend on remembering may be helpful, its causal certainty makes the overall message potentially misleading. Taking memory concerns seriously does not require treating them as confirmation of hidden trauma.
