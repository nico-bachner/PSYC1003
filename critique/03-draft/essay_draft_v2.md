# Does a Memory Gap Mean the Brain Is Protecting Us?

Uncertainty about a possible traumatic experience can make explanations of memory especially consequential: they may shape what people believe happened to them. In Video 1, a speaker identifying as an EMDR therapist argues that this uncertainty usually occurs because the brain blocks an overwhelming memory for protection. Research on trauma and forgetting gives this account some plausibility, but does not establish its central generalisation. Evidence of documented events going unreported and of deliberate memory suppression must be weighed against alternative explanations and limits on applying laboratory findings to personal histories. On this basis, the video's causal explanation exceeds the evidence, despite its potentially reassuring message that improvement need not depend on remembering.

A strong starting point for the speaker's position is evidence that genuine traumatic events are not always reported later. Williams (1994) interviewed 129 women approximately 17 years after documented childhood sexual victimisation; 38% did not report the previously recorded event. Crucially, the earlier records provided evidence independent of adult recollection. Among women aged seven or older when the event occurred, 28% did not report it, so the overall result cannot be attributed entirely to infantile amnesia. These findings challenge the assumption that an event's seriousness guarantees its later recall. However, an interview establishes what someone reports, not necessarily everything she remembers. Selective nondisclosure and ordinary forgetting remain possible, meaning that the findings are compatible with protective blocking without identifying it as the cause.

Evidence about delayed recollections strengthens this qualified affirmative case. Geraerts et al. (2007) recruited adults reporting continuous or recovered memories of childhood sexual abuse and used independent raters to assess corroboration. Supporting evidence was found for 37% of spontaneous recoveries outside therapy, compared with 45% of continuous memories. Thus, delayed recollections cannot reasonably be dismissed as inherently false. Nevertheless, corroborating an event does not explain its earlier apparent absence from memory. Participants' accounts of having forgotten were retrospective, and the self-selected sample cannot establish how common each kind of memory history is. The study supports taking some recovered memories seriously, while leaving the proposed protective mechanism unresolved.

Laboratory research provides a more direct way to investigate whether people can reduce memory accessibility. Across two experiments involving 96 undergraduates, Hulbert and Anderson (2018) asked participants to retrieve or suppress newly learned word pairs. Their combined analysis showed greater suppression-induced forgetting among participants reporting more childhood trauma, although the corresponding interaction in the second experiment alone was not statistically significant. This offers a plausible scientific basis for the idea that keeping memories out of awareness can make them harder to retrieve. Yet trauma history was not experimentally assigned, so the association does not establish that trauma strengthened memory control. More fundamentally, deliberately suppressing recently learned words differs from automatically losing access to an overwhelming autobiographical event. The study also does not demonstrate that such forgetting protects psychological wellbeing.

The relationship between trauma and suppression is further complicated by Catarino et al. (2015). Eighteen participants with posttraumatic stress disorder showed less suppression-induced forgetting than 18 trauma-exposed controls without the disorder, using learned associations between objects and distressing scenes. This finding cautions against assuming that trauma exposure generally produces effective memory blocking. It does not directly contradict Hulbert and Anderson (2018), whose groups were defined by reported trauma history rather than clinical diagnosis. Both studies used laboratory material, and the small clinical comparison leaves causal direction uncertain. Together, they suggest variation in memory control rather than a uniform protective response.

Other cognitive processes may also contribute to experiences described as memory recovery. Geraerts et al. (2009) compared four groups of 30 adults: spontaneous recoverers, people reporting recovery during suggestive therapy, continuous rememberers, and controls reporting no abuse. Spontaneous recoverers more often forgot previous acts of remembering on a laboratory task; the suggestive-therapy group was more susceptible to false word-list memories. These results suggest that apparent recovery can involve different processes, including forgetting that something was remembered before. They do not establish whether participants' abuse memories were accurate or what caused their personal memory histories. Nor can findings about suggestive therapy be attributed to the video speaker, who specifies no such techniques. Their relevance is that protective blocking is not the only account requiring consideration.

Taken together, the affirmative findings establish separate possibilities: an event may go unreported, a delayed recollection may have corroboration, and deliberate suppression may impair recall. Combining these possibilities does not show that an overwhelming event caused protective blocking in an uncertain viewer. Most decisively, none of these studies compares the relative causes of uncertainty in the audience addressed by the video. The word “usually” therefore requires evidence beyond what these findings provide. This is a limit on the claim's justification, not proof that protective blocking never occurs.

For viewers without scientific training, the explanation could turn uncertainty into confidence that hidden trauma occurred. Anderson et al. (2025) found that 93.9% of respondents answering a US online survey item endorsed long-term repression followed by recovery. This indicates a receptive belief context, not evidence that repression is common or that this video changes beliefs. The speaker also explicitly allows that memories may never return and symptoms may nevertheless improve, potentially reducing pressure to remember. Even so, the message is more misleading than helpful as scientific communication overall: its reassurance rests partly on an unsupported explanation of the viewer's past. This judgement concerns the message's evidential quality; the studies did not measure its psychological effects.

The research warrants openness to genuine memory gaps and corroborated delayed recollections, alongside caution about their causes. Suppression experiments offer a possible mechanism, but cannot establish protective blocking as the usual explanation for an uncertain personal history. The video's reassurance has value, yet would be more scientifically defensible if it preserved uncertainty about both the event and the reason it cannot be recalled.
