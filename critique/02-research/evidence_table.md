# Video 1: six-source evidence record

This evidence record covers the six selected local papers and their bearing on the supplied video. Source selection is complete. The selection is bounded, not a systematic literature review. No further literature expansion is needed for the current argument; reopen selection if the claim changes or verification materially changes a retained finding.

The claim concerns whether uncertainty that a traumatic event occurred is **usually caused by protective blocking**. Keep event occurrence, nonreport, memory accessibility, earlier forgetting mechanism, and later-memory accuracy separate. None of these six studies estimates the relative causes of uncertainty in the population addressed by the video.

## Williams (1994)

[Local PDF](sources/Williams-1994.pdf). Scanned journal article, pp. 1167–1176; visual checks of PDF pages 1, 5 and 6 (journal pp. 1167, 1171–1172).

- **Design and findings:** 129 adult women interviewed approximately 17 years after documented childhood sexual victimisation. Forty-nine (38%) did not report the index event. The paper reports 28% nonrecall among women aged seven or older at the event (p. 1171 and Table 1, p. 1172).
- **Bearing on the claim:** Earlier documentation supplies affirmative evidence that actual events can go unreported much later. The older-child result prevents explaining the entire finding through infantile amnesia.
- **Limit:** An interview outcome does not conclusively distinguish blocked access from nondisclosure, ordinary forgetting or other explanations. The author's interpretation of forgetting is not a direct measurement of protective blocking. This cohort does not identify the usual cause among people unsure an event happened.
- **Correction:** The [APA correction record](https://psycnet.apa.org/record/1995-33458-001) for Williams (1995), *Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology, 63*(3), 343, DOI 10.1037/0022-006X.63.3.343, identifies a reversed sentence on p. 1172: women subjected to more force tended to **recall** the abuse, rather than not recall it. The correction does not revise the sample, 38% nonreport result or age-seven-plus result used here. The direction-of-force statement is not used in the current essay plan or historical draft. This is a tendency, not a claim of a statistically significant force effect.


## Hulbert and Anderson (2018)

[Local PDF](sources/Hulbert-Anderson-2018.pdf). Journal pp. 1931–1949; pooled analysis in PDF pages 10–12 and Experiment 2 results in pages 8–9.

- **Design and findings:** Two Think/No-Think experiments, 48 undergraduates each, N = 96 overall. Higher and lower self-reported childhood-trauma groups deliberately retrieved or suppressed newly learned word pairs. The pooled analysis favoured greater suppression-induced forgetting in the higher-trauma group (interaction p = .009); the corresponding interaction in Experiment 2 alone was nonsignificant (p = .122). The authors found no significant corresponding difference between experiments.
- **Bearing on the claim:** Supplies a mechanistic analogy for deliberate retrieval control and a trauma-history association.
- **Limit:** Trauma history was not randomly assigned. New laboratory words under explicit instructions do not establish automatic inaccessibility of autobiographical trauma, a protective benefit, or a causal effect of trauma on control. Preserve the Experiment 2 caveat without treating it as proof of no effect.

## Catarino et al. (2015)

[Local PDF](sources/Catarino-2015.pdf). Advance-publication layout with PDF page numbers 1–13; participants and task on pages 2–4, results and discussion on pages 6–10. Cite the journal publication, pp. 604–616; do not silently treat manuscript page numbers as journal pages.

- **Design and findings:** Eighteen participants with PTSD and 18 trauma-exposed controls without PTSD learned object–aversive-scene pairs. In a deliberate retrieval-suppression task, PTSD participants showed less suppression-induced forgetting than controls.
- **Bearing on the claim:** Qualifies generalisation from trauma exposure to improved memory control.
- **Limit:** Small observational clinical-group comparison using generic, recently learned scenes. PTSD and self-reported trauma-history groups are different populations: this is not a direct failed replication of Hulbert and Anderson. The direction of causation is unresolved. Do not overstate the within-PTSD symptom correlation as an independently significant finding.

## Geraerts et al. (2007)

[Local PDF](sources/Geraerts-2007.pdf). Journal pp. 564–568; methods and results on pp. 565–566, discussion on pp. 566–567.

- **Design and findings:** Newspaper-recruited, self-selected sample: 71 continuous rememberers and 57 discontinuous rememberers (41 spontaneous recoveries outside therapy and 16 recoveries in therapy). Independent raters assessed corroboration. Corroboration was found for 45% of continuous memories, 37% of spontaneous recoveries, and none of the 16 in-therapy recoveries.
- **Bearing on the claim:** Supports independent corroboration of some delayed recollections, alongside continuously remembered events.
- **Limit:** Lack of corroboration does not mean a memory is false. Recovery histories were retrospective, and context was not randomised. Event corroboration neither verifies every detail nor establishes why a memory was previously unreported or apparently unavailable. The therapy comparison does not isolate a causal therapy effect.

## Geraerts et al. (2009)

[Local PDF](sources/Geraerts-2009.pdf). Final journal layout, pp. 92–98; groups and tasks on pp. 93–94, results on pp. 94–96. Author Michelle M. Arnold is **M. M. Arnold** in the reference.

- **Design and findings:** 120 advertisement-recruited adults, four retrospectively classified groups of 30: spontaneous recovery, recovery in suggestive therapy, continuous memory, and no-abuse controls. Laboratory tasks measured false word-list recall/recognition and forgetting earlier acts of remembering. Spontaneous recoverers more often forgot prior laboratory remembering; the suggestive-therapy recovery group showed greater susceptibility to false word-list memories.
- **Bearing on the claim:** Provides possible alternative cognitive correlates of reported recovery, rather than one uniform explanation.
- **Limit:** Laboratory group differences do not establish the cause or truth of participants' autobiographical memories. False word-list recall is not proof of false abuse memories. The specifically suggestive-therapy group cannot represent all therapy, EMDR or the video speaker's practice.

## Anderson et al. (2025)

[Local PDF](sources/Anderson-et-al-2025.pdf). Journal pp. 495–509; recruitment on p. 497 (PDF page 3), belief item on p. 499 (PDF page 5).

- **Design and findings:** US adults recruited through Prolific; 1,634 recruited and 1,581 retained after incomplete surveys were excluded. Wording conditions used direct abuse questions or descriptions of unwanted experiences. For the long-term repression/recovery belief item, 93.9% (n = 1,481 endorsers) agreed. Describe this as a proportion answering that item, rather than deriving it from the full-sample denominator.
- **Bearing on the claim:** Empirical audience-belief context: many respondents endorsed the idea of long-term repression followed by recovery.
- **Limit:** Beliefs and retrospective reports do not demonstrate repression, validate abuse histories or estimate protective-amnesia prevalence. The study did not test this video or its effects. The authors describe the sample as nationally representative; avoid expanding that description into unrestricted generalisation.

## Drafting boundaries

Use Williams and Geraerts (2007) to acknowledge nonreport and some corroborated delayed recollections. Pair the suppression studies to assess the mechanism analogy. Use Geraerts (2009) for possible alternatives and Anderson (2025) for audience context only. Conclude that the usual-cause claim is not established by this set, rather than that blocking is impossible or another explanation is usually correct.

Acknowledge the speaker's allowance that memories may not return and symptoms may improve regardless. Potential reassurance and misleading inferences are reasoned possibilities, not measured treatment or video effects. No retained study justifies diagnosing viewers or attributing suggestive recovery methods to the speaker.

Dalenberg (2012), its reply and Lynn (2014) are excluded from the current six-source set. If expanding the source set, evaluate the original papers.
