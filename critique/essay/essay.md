## Introduction

Over 90% of American adults believe that traumatic memories can be repressed and later recalled. Over 75% also believe unaddressed repressed memories can cause problems in the body. (Anderson et al., 2025) Given the popularity of these beliefs, it is no wonder that both topics are central to the message of the person in the video. If their motivation is widespread viewership and appeal, they have certainly picked their topic wisely. However, it is precisely those motivations that call into question the credibility of the claims they put forward. This essay seeks to verify the statements made by referencing a variety of scientific studies and identify any falsehoods or misleading wording. The thesis put forward is that the claims made by the person will not stand up to scrutiny.

## Affirmative

Catarino et al. (2015) found that people suffering from PTSD actually struggle to suppress their memories when compared to those not afflicted by PTSD. This directly refutes the claim that "the brain [...] blocks access to [a] memory". It is, however, worth noting that the memories suppressed in the study were non-traumatic, so it's not a 1-1 comparison.

Geraerts et al. (2007) provide evidence that suggests traumatic events recalled during therapy are more likely to be false memories than traumatic events recalled outside therapy. Memories of CSA recovered outside therapy were just as likely to be corroborated by external sources than continuous memories (the control). Memories recovered within therapy were never corroborated. This finding strongly suggests that, through a combination of patient expectations and suggestions made by their therapists, therapists are in general harmful to anyone suspecting repressed memories. Geraerts et al. (2009) build on this with their finding that people are more likely to falsely recall a word if they were primed with other similar words. Given this information, we could interpret the person in the video as promoting the use of therapy as promotion for personal gain.

## Negative

Hulbert et al. (2018) found that students with more childhood trauma were more likely to forget learned words. Their findings contradict Catarino et al. (2015). However, the measured difference was of Hubert et al. was less significant than that of Catarino et al.

Williams (1994) found that 38% of women did not report abuse that was documented to have occurred during their childhood. The younger the victim was at the time, the higher the rate of non-report. These results suggest that the victims did not remember those traumatic experiences. However, there are alternative explanations for the results that could undermine the credibility of the claim. Firstly, the victims may simply be uncomfortable disclosing their encounters, while still remembering the experience. There is some evidence pointing towards that not being the case, such as 68% of those women reporting other cases of assault. However, even if we assume non-reports stem from non-recall, at the early age of the women when the abuse occurred, memory is generally less reliable. Again, some evidence refutes this, as 28% of women abused above the age of 7, at which point memory generally stabilises, still did not report. Overall, the findings support the claim that traumatic events prevent remembering the event. However, it does not address the claim that it's protective.

## Critical Analysis

The evidence put forward by the sources consulted is at best inconclusive, and at worst outright disproves some of the claims made by the person in the video. When taking into account the authoritative tone which the speaker adopts, along with their use of their credentials as a licensed medical health counsellor (LMHC), an untrained audience could easily be led to believe the claims put forward. This represents an abuse of authority which, in addition to being morally reproachable, can be harmful to the audience. We already discovered that the use of suggestive language can bring forth false memories. This could easily lead members of the audience to self-diagnose as having had a traumatic experience, leading to feelings of stress and grief where those could easily have been avoided.

There are, however, some redeeming elements of the video. The person states that "either those memories will come back or they won't". The findings of Geraerts et al. (2007) largely support this: memories recovered outside of therapy are corroborated at a similar rate to continuous memories. While the speaker may be furthering their agenda of gaining popularity on social media, they are not explicitly promoting the use of therapy, so their abuse of authority is limited in scope.

## Conclusion

The findings generally contradict the claims made by the person in the video. The person seems motivated by a desire for internet popularity instead of a desire to educate, and are generally unhelpful and even potentially harmful.
