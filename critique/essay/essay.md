## Introduction

Over 90% of American adults believe that traumatic memories can be repressed and later recalled. Over 75% also believe unaddressed repressed memories can cause problems in the body. (Anderson et al., 2025) Given the popularity of these beliefs, it is no wonder that repressed memories are central to the message of the person in Video 1. The speaker argues that the brain usually blocks access to overwhelming traumatic memories because it is protective. However, the popularity of this explanation does not mean that the scientific evidence supports it. This essay seeks to verify the statements made by referencing a variety of scientific studies and identify any falsehoods or misleading wording. The thesis put forward is that, while the studies support some forgetting and memory recovery, they do not show that the brain blocks these memories for protection.


## Affirmative

Hulbert et al. (2018) found that students with more childhood trauma were more likely to forget learned words after deliberately suppressing them. Their findings do not necessarily contradict Catarino et al. (2015), as the two studies looked at different groups of people. It is also worth noting that deliberately suppressing learned words is different from the brain automatically blocking traumatic memories.

Williams (1994) found that 38% of women did not report abuse that was documented to have occurred during their childhood. The abuse had been documented in hospital records before the women were interviewed as adults, so the study did not rely only on their later reports that it had occurred. The younger the victim was at the time, the higher the rate of non-report. These results suggest that the victims did not remember those traumatic experiences. However, there are alternative explanations for the results that could undermine the credibility of the claim. Firstly, the victims may simply be uncomfortable disclosing their encounters, while still remembering the experience. There is some evidence pointing towards that not being the case, such as 68% of those women reporting other cases of assault, although they may still have been uncomfortable discussing the particular incident. However, even if we assume non-reports stem from non-recall, at the early age of the women when the abuse occurred, memory is generally less reliable. However, non-reporting was not limited to early childhood: 28% of women abused at age seven or older still did not report. Overall, the findings show that people may not report traumatic events that are known to have occurred. However, this does not show that they forgot those events or that the forgetting was protective.


## Negative

Catarino et al. (2015) found that people suffering from post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) actually struggle to suppress their memories when compared to trauma-exposed people not afflicted by PTSD. This does not directly refute the claim that the brain automatically blocks access to traumatic memories. The study tested deliberate suppression of recently learned unpleasant pictures, rather than automatic blocking of participants’ own traumatic experiences.

Geraerts et al. (2007) provide evidence that suggests memories recovered during therapy are less likely to be corroborated than memories recovered outside therapy. Memories of CSA recovered outside therapy were corroborated at a rate that did not differ significantly from continuous memories (the control). Corroboration was found for 45% of continuous memories, 37% of memories recovered outside therapy, and none of those recovered within therapy, a group of 16 participants. This finding raises concerns that patient expectations and suggestions made by therapists could contribute to false memories. However, the lack of corroboration does not prove that the memories were false, and the study does not show that therapy caused harm. Geraerts et al. (2009) build on this with their finding that people reporting recovery during suggestive therapy were more likely than comparison groups to falsely recall related words that had not been presented. However, making errors on this word task does not show that their memories of abuse were false. People reporting spontaneous recovery were also more likely to forget that they had recalled a word earlier. This suggests that people may feel they have recovered a memory because they have forgotten remembering it before, rather than because it was blocked.


## Critical Analysis

The evidence put forward by the sources consulted does not show that the brain blocks traumatic memories for protection, as claimed by the person in the video. When taking into account the authoritative tone which the speaker adopts, an untrained audience could easily be led to believe the claims put forward. Presenting this explanation as a fact could be harmful to the audience. The studies discussed raise concerns about suggestion and memory errors. The video could lead members of the audience to self-diagnose as having had a traumatic experience, leading to feelings of stress and grief that might otherwise have been avoided. However, the studies discussed did not test whether the video has this effect.

There are, however, some redeeming elements of the video. The person states that "either those memories will come back or they won't". The findings of Geraerts et al. (2007) also suggest that some recovered memories can be accurate: memories recovered outside of therapy are corroborated at a similar rate to continuous memories. The speaker also states that symptoms can improve even if the memories do not return, which may be reassuring. However, the sources consulted do not test whether this is the case.


## Conclusion

The findings support some forgetting and memory recovery, but do not show that the brain blocks traumatic memories for protection. The explanation given by the person in the video may therefore be misleading and potentially harmful, even if the suggestion that memories do not need to return is reassuring.
