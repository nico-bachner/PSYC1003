# Evidence list

Summary of the source PDFs for use in essay preparation.

## Glossary

- **Retrieval:** bringing a memory to mind.
- **Suppression:** deliberately trying to stop a memory from coming to mind.
- **Repression:** the proposed process in which distressing memories are automatically kept outside conscious awareness. This differs from deliberate suppression.
- **Recovered memory:** a memory that someone reports remembering after a period of not remembering it. This label does not establish why it was forgotten or whether it is accurate.

## 1. Williams (1994): later reports of documented childhood abuse

**Article:** Linda Meyer Williams, _Recall of childhood trauma: A prospective study of women's memories of child sexual abuse_. Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology, 62(6), 1167–1176.

**Source:** [Article PDF, including its correction](../sources/Williams-1994.pdf). See pp. 1169–1174; the correction is on PDF page 11.

### Contents and method

Williams investigated whether adults reported childhood sexual abuse that had been documented roughly 17 years earlier. The final analysis included **129 women**, whose original hospital records concerned abuse at ages ranging from 10 months to 12 years. Interviewers asked detailed questions about childhood experiences but did not tell the women the details of the recorded incident. Researchers compared the interviews with the earlier records.

This is a useful design because evidence of the childhood event existed before the adult interview. It does not rely solely on an adult's claim that an event occurred.

### Key findings

- **49 of 129 women (38%) did not report the documented abuse or other sexual abuse by the same perpetrator.** The article interprets this as evidence of non-recall, although the directly measured outcome was non-reporting during the interview.
- Of those who did not report the documented incident, **68% reported other childhood sexual assaults**. This weakens the explanation that all non-reporting reflected a general unwillingness to discuss abuse, but does not rule out reluctance to disclose a particular incident.
- Younger age at the time of abuse was associated with non-reporting. Women abused by someone they knew were also more likely not to report the event than those abused by a stranger.
- Non-reporting was not limited to very early childhood: **28% of women abused at age seven or older** did not report the documented abuse.

### Limits and relevance to the video

The study supports the narrower point that a documented traumatic event may be absent from a later account. It does **not** demonstrate that the brain automatically blocked the memory for protection. Non-disclosure, ordinary forgetting, early childhood memory development, and the way the interview prompted recall remain relevant explanations. Participants were not directly reminded of the specific recorded event, so the study could not test whether that reminder would restore recall.

Only part of the original group was interviewed, and the sample consisted mainly of African American women from a poor urban community whose abuse had reached hospital services. The results may not generalise to all survivors. The study also did not test the accuracy of later recovered memories or the effectiveness of therapy.

**Correction to preserve:** The appended correction reverses a sentence on p. 1172: greater force showed a _tendency towards recall_, not non-recall. The reported comparison was not conventionally statistically significant (p = .059), so it should not be presented as firm evidence that force improves recall.

**Possible essay use:** Evidence that non-reporting of documented trauma occurs, paired with the caution that non-reporting does not establish protective repression.

## 2. Geraerts et al. (2007): independent support for recovered memories

**Article:** Elke Geraerts and colleagues, _The reality of recovered memories: Corroborating continuous and discontinuous memories of childhood sexual abuse_. Psychological Science, 18(7), 564–567.

**Source:** [Article PDF](../sources/Geraerts-2007.pdf), especially pp. 565–567.

### Contents and method

Researchers recruited **128 adults** through newspaper advertisements. They compared people who said they had always remembered their abuse (**71**), recovered memories outside therapy (**41**), or recovered memories in therapy (**16**).

Two assessors, who did not know participants' memory-group membership, sought corroboration. This could include someone who learned of the abuse within a week of the event, another person reporting abuse by the same perpetrator, or a perpetrator's admission.

### Key findings

- Corroboration was found for **45%** of the continuous-memory group, **37%** of the group reporting recovery outside therapy, and **0%** of the group reporting recovery in therapy.
- The continuous-memory and outside-therapy groups did not differ significantly in their corroboration rates.
- People reporting recovery outside therapy described greater surprise at remembering. Greater surprise was also associated with a higher likelihood of corroboration.

### Limits and relevance to the video

The study supports the possibility that some apparently recovered memories concern real events. It also raises concerns about expectations and suggestion during attempts to recover memories.

However, **failure to find corroboration does not prove that a memory is false**. The therapy group was small, and corroboration itself was imperfect: another person's abuse by the same perpetrator does not verify every detail of the participant's experience. The study did not randomly assign people to therapy, so it cannot establish that therapy caused false memories. Reports of having previously forgotten the abuse were also retrospective.

Even when an event is corroborated, this does not show that the memory was previously inaccessible because of repression. A person may have avoided thinking about it or forgotten earlier occasions of remembering.

**Possible essay use:** Support for the possibility of accurate recovered memories, alongside caution about treating the recovery experience as proof of either accuracy or protective blocking.

## 3. Geraerts et al. (2009): different explanations for memory recovery

**Article:** Elke Geraerts and colleagues, _Cognitive mechanisms underlying recovered-memory experiences of childhood sexual abuse_. Psychological Science, 20(1), 92–98.

**Source:** [Article PDF](../sources/Geraerts-2009.pdf), especially pp. 93–97 and Tables 1–2.

### Contents and method

The researchers compared **120 adults**, with 30 in each of four groups: memories recovered during suggestive therapy, memories recovered spontaneously outside therapy, continuous memories of abuse, and no reported abuse history.

Participants completed two laboratory tasks. One tested false memories for words: after studying related words such as “bed” and “tired”, people might incorrectly remember seeing “sleep”. The other tested whether people remembered having recalled a word earlier, especially when the clues used to prompt recall changed.

The suggestive-therapy group specifically reported techniques such as hypnosis, guided imagery, or dream interpretation during attempts to reconstruct their past.

### Key findings

- The suggestive-therapy group falsely recalled and recognised more related but unpresented words than each of the other three groups. Correct memory for words actually presented was similar across groups.
- The spontaneous-recovery group was more likely to forget that it had already recalled a word when the retrieval context changed. For words recalled successfully on both tests under changed cues, this group correctly recognised its earlier recall on **24%** of trials, compared with **52–56%** in the other groups.
- The spontaneous-recovery group did not show greater susceptibility to false memories on the word-list task than the continuous-memory or control groups.

### Limits and relevance to the video

The findings suggest more than one explanation for feeling that a memory has returned. Some people may misremember information; others may accurately remember an event but forget that they have remembered it before. The second possibility challenges the assumption that a memory that feels newly recovered must previously have been completely blocked.

These were laboratory word tasks, not tests of the truth of participants' abuse accounts. Group differences cannot determine whether an individual's memory is accurate. The design also cannot establish whether suggestive therapy caused greater susceptibility to false memories or whether that susceptibility existed beforehand. It does not represent every form of therapy or spontaneous remembering during therapy.

**Possible essay use:** Evidence for alternative explanations that the video's single protective-blocking account leaves out.

## 4. Catarino et al. (2015): difficulty suppressing memories in PTSD

**Article:** Ana Catarino and colleagues, _Failing to forget: Inhibitory-control deficits compromise memory suppression in posttraumatic stress disorder_. Psychological Science. DOI: 10.1177/0956797615569889.

**Source:** [Article PDF](../sources/Catarino-2015.pdf), especially PDF pp. 2–6 and 9–10. This copy uses advance-publication pagination.

### Contents and method

Researchers compared **18 adults with PTSD** with **18 trauma-exposed adults without current or past PTSD**. Participants learned pairs of objects and unpleasant pictures. In a “think/no-think” task, object cues instructed them either to recall the associated picture or deliberately prevent it from entering awareness. Other pictures were left unpractised as a baseline for comparison.

A final test measured whether participants could identify each picture and recall its main content and details.

### Key findings

- Participants with PTSD showed less forgetting after suppression than the comparison group across all three memory measures.
- For the main content of pictures, suppression reduced recall relative to baseline by **10.8 percentage points** in the comparison group, versus **1.7 points** in the PTSD group. Only the comparison group's reduction was statistically significant.
- The groups were similar in initial learning and baseline memory. This suggests that the difference concerned controlling retrieval rather than a general inability to learn the material.
- Within the PTSD group, the relationship between symptom severity and poorer suppression was tentative: it was not conventionally significant before accounting for depression scores (p = .07), but just reached that threshold after adjustment (p = .049).

### Limits and relevance to the video

The study suggests that deliberate control can reduce access to some recently learned material. It also illustrates a different trauma-related problem from the one emphasised in the video: difficulty keeping unwanted memories out of awareness.

The sample was small, and participants suppressed pictures learned in a laboratory, not their own traumatic experiences. The study cannot determine whether difficulties with suppression preceded PTSD or developed afterwards. It did not measure brain activity, test automatic protective repression, or test a treatment. Proposed brain mechanisms and clinical applications are interpretations, not direct results of this experiment.

**Possible essay use:** Evidence that memory control varies between people, and that trauma-related memory difficulties can involve persistent recall rather than blocked access.

## 5. Hulbert and Anderson (2018): trauma history and deliberate memory control

**Article:** Justin C. Hulbert and Michael C. Anderson, _What doesn't kill you makes you stronger: Psychological trauma and its relationship to enhanced memory control_. Journal of Experimental Psychology: General. DOI: 10.1037/xge0000461.

**Source:** [Article PDF](../sources/Hulbert-Anderson-2018.pdf), especially PDF pp. 3, 7–8 and 10–17. This copy uses advance-publication pagination.

### Contents and method

Two experiments each analysed **48 university students**. Students reported traumatic experiences before age 18 and were grouped by higher or lower reported trauma exposure. They learned word pairs containing neutral or negative target words, then repeatedly recalled or suppressed selected targets.

Final tests used either the original cue or a different clue to the same target. The different-clue test helped assess whether forgetting extended beyond the particular cue used during suppression. In the second experiment, researchers measured trauma history beforehand, kept experimenters unaware of group membership, and offered money for correct recall.

### Key findings

- Across the experiments, students reporting more trauma showed greater suppression-related forgetting. The pattern occurred for both neutral and negative words.
- The clearest group difference concerned recall with different clues, suggesting that the effect extended beyond the original learned association.
- For example, in Experiment 2's different-clue test, the higher-trauma group recalled **59% of baseline words and 50% of repeatedly suppressed words**. The lower-trauma group recalled **51% in both conditions** (Table 1).
- The overall pattern remained when experimenters were unaware of trauma history and participants were rewarded for remembering.

### Limits and relevance to the video

This supports the limited claim that deliberately preventing retrieval can make some memories harder to recall later. The authors propose that coping with unwanted memories may provide practice in memory control.

However, trauma history was measured rather than experimentally assigned. The study cannot show that trauma improved memory control. Students with stronger pre-existing control may have been more likely to cope successfully and enter the university sample. Trauma reports were not independently verified, and the researchers lacked detailed information about severity and PTSD history.

The tasks involved recently learned words, not forgotten traumatic events. They do not establish automatic protection, complete loss of autobiographical memory, or the later recovery of accurate memories. The title should not be read as a general finding that trauma makes people stronger.

**Possible essay use:** Qualified support for a mechanism that can reduce memory accessibility, while clearly distinguishing voluntary suppression from the video's proposed automatic response.

## 6. Anderson et al. (2025): public beliefs about repression

**Article:** Kris-Ann S. Anderson, Quincy C. Miller, Deryn Strange, and Kamala London, _Repressed memories and the body keeps the score: Public perceptions and prevalence_. Memory, 33(5), 495–509. DOI: 10.1080/09658211.2025.2479503.

**Source:** [Article PDF](../sources/Anderson-et-al-2025.pdf), especially pp. 497–505 and Table 2. This paper's first author is different from Michael C. Anderson in the preceding studies.

### Contents and method

The researchers surveyed **1,581 American adults** in a sample designed to represent the adult population. They asked about beliefs in repression, experiences of memory recovery, and the circumstances surrounding those experiences. They also compared direct questions about abuse with questions describing unwanted experiences, and analysed written accounts.

### Key findings

- **93.5%** agreed that traumatic memories can be repressed, and **93.9%** agreed that they can be repressed for years and later recovered. Agreement included responses from “slightly agree” to “strongly agree”.
- The paper reports **77.3%** agreement that unaddressed repressed memories can cause problems in the body. This measures belief in that proposition, not evidence that it is true.
- **57 respondents (3.6%)** reported recovering abuse memories for which they previously had neither knowledge nor memory. A broader category, also including people who previously knew of abuse but reported no memory of it, contained **114 respondents (7.2%)**.
- Follow-up questions showed that some initial reports of recovery instead involved continuous memories or later recognising an already remembered event as abuse. Question wording affected the pattern of responses.
- Some written accounts described deliberately avoiding thoughts, ordinary forgetting, or other experiences that do not necessarily imply unconscious repression.

### Limits and relevance to the video

The study shows that beliefs resembling the video's explanation were widespread in this sample. It also shows why clear definitions matter: people may use “repression” to describe quite different experiences.

The researchers deliberately did not define repression before the belief questions, so agreement does not mean every respondent endorsed the same mechanism. Self-reported recovered memories were not independently verified. The authors also acknowledge that survey filtering and differences between written accounts and fixed-choice answers complicate prevalence estimates.

This survey cannot establish whether repression occurs, whether any particular memory is accurate, or whether social media caused these beliefs. References to possible media influence should be presented as interpretation rather than an experimentally demonstrated effect.

**Possible essay use:** Context for assessing how a general audience might understand the video's message, especially the risk of treating avoidance or gaps in memory as proof of hidden trauma.

| Question                                                                              | Most relevant sources                             | What the evidence allows us to say                                                             |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Can documented trauma be absent from a later account?                                 | Williams (1994)                                   | Yes, but non-reporting does not identify its cause.                                            |
| Can apparently recovered memories concern real events?                                | Geraerts et al. (2007)                            | Some have corroboration; this does not establish prior repression or verify every detail.      |
| Are there other explanations for a recovery experience?                               | Geraerts et al. (2009); Anderson et al. (2025)    | Forgetting earlier remembering, reinterpretation, and memory errors are relevant alternatives. |
| Can deliberate suppression reduce later recall?                                       | Catarino et al. (2015); Hulbert & Anderson (2018) | Yes, under some laboratory conditions, with substantial differences between groups.            |
| Does trauma usually cause automatic protective blocking?                              | Consider the six studies together                 | These studies do not establish that mechanism or show it is the usual explanation.             |
| Does the video's treatment approach improve symptoms with or without memory recovery? | Not directly tested by these six sources          | Treatment-outcome research would be needed to evaluate this separate claim.                    |

The two suppression papers study different populations and need not contradict each other: one compares adults with and without PTSD, while the other compares university students by reported trauma history. Neither measures the automatic loss of access to a personal traumatic memory. Likewise, public belief in repression is evidence about beliefs, not evidence that repression explains memory gaps.
